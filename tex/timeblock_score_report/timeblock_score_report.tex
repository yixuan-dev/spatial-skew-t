\begin{abstract}
Two clean experiments compare the AR(2) skew-t model against the
i.i.d.-in-time skew-t baseline under the time-block forecast protocol, by
CRPS and by the Brier score at the $q_{95}$ threshold, resolved by forecast
lead time. \textbf{Experiment A} (setting 4, five expanding-window blocks
$\times$ 5 datasets): the models tie once the reflected-ridge blocks are
excluded; the only visible AR(2) gain sits at lead 1 and is gone by lead 3,
exactly as the geometric memory of $\phi = (0.80, -0.35)$ predicts.
\textbf{Experiment B} (block 1 only, guarded fits, datasets 1--10): at the
same setting 4 the tie replicates; at the near-unit-root setting 5,
$\phi = (0.15, 0.80)$, the AR(2) advantage becomes real and large --- CRPS
$-31\%$ over leads 1--5 (Wilcoxon $p = 0.040$; sensitivity $p = 0.010$--$0.021$)
and a Brier improvement that is \emph{flat in lead time} (AR(2) better at 14
of 15 leads, mean $-0.008$), consistent with a memory horizon
($h^\ast \approx 109$ days) that dwarfs the 15-day window.
\textbf{Experiment C} (block 1, two long-memory settings, ARFIMA$(0,d,0)$ with
hyperbolic-decay dependence the AR(2) model cannot represent): the advantage
survives at the stronger $d = 0.45$ (Hurst $0.95$) --- CRPS $-16\%$ at leads
1--5, AR(2) better at 12 of 15 Brier leads --- but is diluted by a short-window
identification failure that flags half the datasets, and vanishes at
$d = 0.35$. Across all four settings the advantage tracks the ratio of memory
horizon to training-window length, not the Hurst exponent, and remains
bounded by the score-ceiling
$\Delta\mathrm{CRPS}/\mathrm{CRPS} = 1 - \sqrt{1 - \mathrm{SNR}\,\kappa_z(h)}$.
Adding an AR(1) arm (settings 5, 7, each filled to clean $n = 10$) decomposes
the gain, and the two regimes split oppositely. At the lag-2-dominant
setting 5, AR(1) is indistinguishable from i.i.d.\ and the entire advantage is
the \emph{second} lag (AR(2)$-$AR(1) CRPS $-0.66$ at leads 1--5,
$W\,p = 0.033$; $-0.40$ at 1--15, $W\,p = 0.021$). Under long memory
(setting 7) the split reverses: AR(1) already captures most of the advantage
(AR(1)$-$i.i.d.\ $-0.26$, $t\,p = 0.044$) and the second lag adds only a
marginal increment ($t\,p = 0.081$, $W\,p = 0.111$) --- the geometric first
lag approximates the hyperbolic decay, and the second buys little. (An earlier
$n = 5$ sample overstated this second-lag increment; the $n = 10$ fill
corrects it.)
\emph{Note (2026-07-31): every Brier figure in this revision uses the
per-block threshold rule since demoted as look-ahead; see the Protocol
paragraph. CRPS results are unaffected.}
\end{abstract}

\noindent Settings 4--5 are short-memory ($\sum_h |\rho(h)| < \infty$, ACF
$\sim \rho(F)^h$); settings 6--7 are genuinely long-memory
($\sum_h |\rho(h)| = \infty$, ACF $\sim h^{2d-1}$), which no finite-order AR
reproduces --- so the AR(2) model is \emph{misspecified by construction} there.
Experiments A and B use settings 4--5; Experiment C uses 6--7.

\paragraph{Protocol.} Fit on a contiguous prefix $y[, 1\!:\!T_o]$ (all sites
observed), forecast the window $(T_o, T_o + 15]$ with the seam-conditioned
recursion of \code{forecast\_block()}. Scores per lead $h$: CRPS
(\code{crps\_sample}, averaged over sites) and the Brier score for exceedance
of a high quantile, identical thresholds for both methods, paired by dataset.

\emph{Threshold rule (revised 2026-07-31).} The scorer now defines the Brier
thresholds as quantiles of the dataset's \emph{full series},
$\mathrm{quantile}(y[,\,,\mathrm{set},\mathrm{setting}],\,p)$ --- the same
rule as the spatial simstudy. The rule used in \emph{every table of this
revision} was the validation block's own quantile,
$\mathrm{quantile}(y_{\mathrm{val}},p)$; it is retained in the score caches
as \code{brier.lead.blockq} but demoted to a diagnostic, for two reasons.
It is look-ahead (the threshold is a statistic of the very window being
forecast), and it pins the realised exceedance rate at exactly $1-p$ in
every block, so a level error cannot move the base rate: on setting 5's
terminal near-unit-root excursion (dataset 2, block 5, observations at
$2.4\times$ the training level) all three methods assign
$\hat p \le 4\times10^{-4}$ to exceedance and score within $0.0005$ of one
another --- while CRPS separates them by a factor of two. Under the
full-series rule the same cell separates the methods decisively, and the
setting-5 $q_{95}$ Brier ordering \emph{inverts} (AR(2) best instead of
i.i.d.). The cost is coverage: at high thresholds a quiet block has no
exceedance at all under a fixed threshold ($\approx 80\%$ of
(lead, quantile, dataset, block) cells at setting 5, $\approx 62\%$ at
setting 7, recorded per cell in \code{exceed.rate.lead}), and there the
score degenerates onto climatology; the level-error ledger
\code{pexceed.mean} vs.\ \code{exceed.rate.lead} makes this visible. All
Brier tables below therefore reflect the \emph{old} rule and will be
superseded when the settings-5/7 campaign is re-scored; the CRPS columns
are unaffected. Caches record the rule in
\code{brier\_threshold\_basis}, and the merge and sanity gates refuse to
mix rules.

Both experiments post-date the \code{z.init} repair; all
Experiment-B fits passed the pre-registered consistency guard (truth
recovery $\wedge$ predictive spread; see the companion note
\code{tex/guard\_effect}), which replaced 13 of 40 attempt-0 chains.
Experiment A excludes the four blocks flagged by the same assertions
(reflected-ridge fits; \code{tex/lambda\_phiz\_ridge}).

\section{Experiment C: long-memory settings, block 1, guarded, datasets 1--10}

The two long-memory settings replace the AR(2) latent dynamics with
ARFIMA$(0, d, 0)$, whose autocorrelation decays hyperbolically
($\rho(h) \sim h^{2d-1}$) rather than geometrically. No finite-order AR
reproduces this, so the fitted AR(2) model is misspecified by construction;
the question is whether its geometric-memory pooling still beats i.i.d.\ when
the truth has heavier-than-geometric memory. Same block-1 protocol and guard.
The short training window interacts badly with long memory: the guard flags
$2$ of $10$ datasets at $d = 0.35$ and $5$ of $10$ at $d = 0.45$ (both methods,
$\lambda$ stable but under-identified in a $50$-day window), so the clean $n$
is smaller here --- itself a finding about fitting long memory on short records.

\section{Reading the tables}

\paragraph{1. The advantage is a dose response --- in memory relative to the
window, not in the Hurst exponent.} Pooled CRPS gap (leads 1--5): $-0.058$
(setting 4, $\rho(F) = 0.59$), $-0.805$ (setting 5, $\rho(F) = 0.97$,
three-way $n = 10$), $-0.022$ (setting 6, $d = 0.35$), $-0.364$
(setting 7, $d = 0.45$, three-way $n = 10$). The order
is \emph{not} monotone in nominal long-memory strength: the short-memory
near-unit-root setting 5 beats both genuinely long-memory settings. What
orders the four is the memory horizon measured against the $50$-day training
window. Setting 5's $h^\ast \approx 109$ days makes the seam state highly
predictive across the whole $15$-day window; setting 7's hyperbolic ACF is
strong but the AR(2) model can only capture its geometric part, and the
$50$-day window under-identifies even that (see point 4). Setting 6's
short-lag ACF ($\rho(1) \approx 0.4$) sits in the same undetectable band as
setting 4. The ceiling $\Delta\mathrm{CRPS}/\mathrm{CRPS} = 1 - \sqrt{1 -
\mathrm{SNR}\,\kappa_z(h)}$ still bounds every case: the seam state is worth
only what the \emph{fitted} model's $\kappa_z(h)$ retains of it.

\paragraph{2. The lead profile matches the memory (read on CRPS, not Brier).}
Setting 4 ($h^\ast = 6$ d): the CRPS gain lives at leads 1--2 and dies by
lead 3, in \emph{both} experiments (Tables \ref{tab:expA}, \ref{tab:expB4};
lead-1 gaps $-0.308$, $-0.275$ --- a clean cross-protocol replication).
Settings 5 and 7 (long horizon): the AR(2)$-$i.i.d.\ CRPS gain does \emph{not}
decay inside the window --- it persists across leads 1--15, as a long-memory
latent state remains informative $15$ days out. The $q_{95}$ Brier tells the
same story only weakly (setting 7 AR(2) better at 12/15 leads, setting 5 mixed
at 6/15): the tail Brier is too noisy per lead to resolve a profile at
$n = 10$, which is why CRPS is the reported endpoint.

\paragraph{3. The two regimes split the advantage oppositely between the
lags.} The three-way tables (settings 5, 7, each clean $n = 10$) split
AR(2)$-$i.i.d.\ into AR(1)$-$i.i.d.\ (what one lag buys) and AR(2)$-$AR(1)
(the second lag). \emph{Setting 5 (lag-2-dominant, $\phi = (0.15, 0.80)$):}
AR(1) is nearly indistinguishable from i.i.d.\ (CRPS gap $-0.144$, leads
1--5) --- a single lag cannot represent a lag-2-dominant process --- so the
advantage is essentially all second lag (AR(2)$-$AR(1) $= -0.661$ at 1--5,
$W\,p = 0.033$; $-0.397$ at 1--15, $W\,p = 0.021$), now significant at both
windows with the $n = 10$ fill. \emph{Setting 7 (long memory):} the split
reverses. The geometric first lag already approximates the hyperbolic decay,
so AR(1) carries most of the advantage (AR(1)$-$i.i.d.\ $= -0.259$,
$t\,p = 0.044$) and the second lag adds only a marginal increment
(AR(2)$-$AR(1) $= -0.105$, $t\,p = 0.081$, $W\,p = 0.111$). An earlier $n = 5$
sample had put this second-lag increment among the study's sharpest signals
($W\,p = 0.030$); the $n = 10$ fill shows that was an artifact of the small,
favourably-selected sample. Either way the ordering i.i.d.\ $\succeq$ AR(1)
$\succeq$ AR(2) holds, and the AR(2)$-$AR(1) gap localizes what the extra lag
is worth --- decisive under lag-2 structure, marginal under geometric-like
long memory.

\paragraph{4. CRPS detects what Brier cannot, even at $n = 10$.} Where the
advantage is real (settings 5 and 7) the two scores agree in direction but
CRPS reaches significance first: setting 5 CRPS $W\,p = 0.021$--$0.033$
(AR(2)$-$AR(1) and AR(2)$-$i.i.d.), setting 7 CRPS $W\,p = 0.051$
(AR(2)$-$i.i.d.), while \emph{every} Brier endpoint stays $p > 0.1$. The
$q_{95}$ Brier scores one binary tail event per site-day and carries an order
of magnitude more sampling noise; filling from $n = 5/7$ to $n = 10$ sharpened
the CRPS contrasts but left Brier non-significant throughout. Powering the
Brier endpoint to significance at these gaps would need substantially more
than $10$ clean datasets --- roughly $30$--$50$
(\code{run\_block1\_guarded.R --datasets=25:80}).

\paragraph{5. Long memory on a short window is an identification problem, not
just a modelling one --- and the clean-$n$ selection conditions on it.}
Setting 7 flags half its datasets: a $50$-day window can realise a nearly flat
draw of a long-memory $z$ process, which under-identifies $\lambda$ (stable
but too small), and the AR(2) misspecification compounds it. To reach clean
$n = 10$ three-way (all of i.i.d., AR(1), AR(2) passing the guard) the fill
scanned $24$ datasets for setting 7 and $16$ for setting 5. The reported
comparison is therefore \emph{conditional on identifiability}: it answers
``when the model is identified on a $50$-day window, what is each lag worth,''
not ``how often is it identified'' (a separate, roughly-even coin for
setting 7). This selection is not random missingness, and the honest reading
is the conditional one. The direct test of whether long memory simply needs a
longer window is block 5 (train $170$ days), where identification should be
far easier --- the natural next experiment, and more informative than yet more
$50$-day datasets.

\paragraph{6. What the ties at settings 4 and 6 mean.} Neither is evidence
that AR(2) pooling is useless; each says the process memory is short relative
to the $15$-day window, so most scored leads lie beyond it and any pooled
endpoint dilutes its own signal. The informative settings are the ones whose
horizon exceeds the window (5 and 7), which is where the advantage appears.
