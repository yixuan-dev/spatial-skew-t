\section*{摘要}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{摘要}

在環境風險治理中，決策關心的通常不是平均濃度，而是未設站位置超越高門檻的機率。本文以 Morris et al. (2017) 的時空 \skewt 過程 (STP) 為基準，系統檢驗其可改進空間：在時間軸上，將潛在參數的 AR(1) 先驗擴充為符合 Yule--Walker 標準化條件的 AR(2) 先驗；在空間軸上，於迴歸均值加入多尺度薄板樣條 (MRTS) 基底，以提升大尺度空間變異的表達能力。評估分為模擬研究與實證分析。模擬研究涵蓋四組固定 AR(2) 生成設定與一組刻意設計的非定態隨機效應情境；實證分析使用 2005 年 7 月美國 AQS 臭氧資料與 CMAQ 共變數。模型比較以樣本外 Brier 分數為核心指標，聚焦門檻超標預測表現。結果顯示，AR(2) 延伸在多數情境下未帶來穩定增益，且參數可識別性有限；MRTS 延伸僅在部分門檻與設定出現零散改善，未形成一致優勢。整體而言，兩項延伸的主要價值不在於取代 STP 基準，而在於將其擴展為可由樣本外門檻分數進行資料驅動選擇的候選模型類。

\textbf{關鍵字}：時空 \skewt 過程、門檻超標預測、AR(2) 先驗、多尺度薄板樣條、Brier 分數、臭氧

\section*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Abstract}

In environmental risk regulation, the inferential target is often not the field
mean but the probability of exceeding a high threshold at unmonitored
locations. We take the spatio-temporal \skewt process (STP) of Morris et al.
(2017) as baseline and probe where predictive improvement is still possible
along two axes. On the temporal axis, we extend the latent AR(1) prior to a
standardized AR(2) prior constrained by Yule--Walker moments. On the spatial
axis, we augment the regression mean with multi-resolution thin-plate spline
(MRTS) basis functions to capture large-scale spatial variation. Evaluation
combines simulation and real-data analysis. The simulation study includes four
fixed AR(2) generating settings and a deliberately constructed nonstationary
random-effect scenario. The real-data study uses July 2005 U.S. AQS ozone
observations with CMAQ as covariates. Models are compared by held-out Brier
scores focused on threshold exceedance prediction. Across settings, AR(2)
provides little stable gain and shows limited coefficient recoverability; MRTS
yields occasional improvements but no uniform advantage. Thus the main
contribution is to enlarge the STP baseline into a candidate model class
selected by held-out threshold predictive performance.

\textbf{Keywords}: spatio-temporal \skewt process; threshold exceedance prediction; AR(2) prior; multi-resolution thin-plate splines; Brier score; ozone

\section{Introduction}
\pagenumbering{arabic}                  % 阿拉伯數字
\setcounter{page}{1}                    % 將頁碼重設為 1
\WatermarkOn                            % 浮水印

The need for accurate pollution-concentration estimates over broad spatial
domains keeps growing. Epidemiological evidence has tied air quality to
morbidity and mortality, and regulation has tightened. Direct measurement alone
cannot meet this need: the cost and logistics of a monitoring network cap its
spatial coverage and temporal resolution. This gap motivates statistical methods
that extend a network's spatial reach with quantified confidence
\parencite{epa2024downscaler}. Ground-level ozone is the canonical regulatory
case. The National Ambient Air Quality Standard (NAAQS) specifies the maximum
concentration of ozone (O$_3$) allowed in outdoor air: a site complies only if
the three-year average of its annual fourth-highest daily maximum eight-hour
concentration stays at or below the current NAAQS of $0.070$ parts per million
($70$ ppb), last revised in 2015. The Clean Air Act (CAA) requires the EPA to
review it at least every five years. The standards for ozone are shown in Table
1. The policy-relevant target is thus not the mean field
but its upper tail, namely the probability that an unmonitored location breaches
a high threshold. We pursue two goals: spatial prediction at unmonitored sites,
and mapping the probability of extreme exceedances across the domain.

A spatial model for ozone threshold exceedances warrants special consideration,
since standard spatial methods are likely to perform poorly. Two features of the
problem are decisive. First, likelihood-based spatial modeling typically assumes
a Gaussian process, which is appropriate when interest lies in mean behavior. The
Gaussian distribution is light-tailed and symmetric. Daily ozone maxima are
neither. Their residuals are heavy-tailed and are well described by a \skewt
distribution \parencite{morris_space-time_2017}. Second, our interest is in the
dependence structure at high levels, which a covariance designed for mean
behavior does not capture. A Gaussian process is asymptotically independent
outside the degenerate case of perfect dependence, so the probability that two
sites are jointly extreme decays to zero as the level increases. Nearby ozone
monitors, however, tend to record high values on the same days, and this
agreement weakens with distance \parencite{morris_space-time_2017}. These
considerations call for a heavy-tailed, asymmetric margin that retains asymptotic
dependence. The \skewt distribution provides both. It is a subasymptotic model,
capturing the joint tail decay at high but finite levels rather than the limiting
dependence of extreme events \parencite{huser_advances_2022}. This is the regime
the ozone data occupy.

Our approach also departs from threshold models based on extreme-value
distributions, a well-developed part of the field in which extreme events are
defined as exceedances over a high threshold. \textcite{Davison1990} model
univariate exceedances with the generalized Pareto distribution.
\textcite{Ledford1996} address the multivariate case through a censored
likelihood that distinguishes the ways in which a threshold may be exceeded.
\textcite{Wadsworth2012} and \textcite{Thibaud2013} carried these ideas to
spatial extremes, fitting models by a censored pairwise likelihood
\parencite{Padoan2010}. \textcite{Huser2014} extended them to space-time data.
Such methods are grounded in extreme-value theory and presume a threshold high
enough for extremal models to be valid and for extrapolation beyond the observed
range. They are also computationally demanding and, in practice, restricted to
small datasets. Neither feature suits the present application. The 75 ppb
threshold corresponds to roughly the 0.92 sample quantile of the daily ozone
maxima \parencite{morris_space-time_2017}, well short of the far tail that
extremal models require. The network is also large, with $1{,}089$ monitoring
stations. A complementary strategy retains the max-stable process while
lightening its computational cost. Fully Bayesian inference for max-stable
processes is cumbersome at scale. \textcite{morris_exploration_2019} address this
by truncating the spectral representation to a small set of empirical basis
functions that carry the spatial dependence. Their construction renders inference
feasible for large datasets and, as a by-product, dispenses with the assumption
of stationarity.

In this paper we build on the spatial \skewt process of
\textcite{morris_space-time_2017}. This process is not max-stable, yet it retains
extremal dependence, and it is constructed by location-scale mixing of Gaussian
processes. Their model has three components. A random spatial partition, redrawn each day,
confines asymptotic dependence to nearby sites. A censored likelihood focuses
inference on exceedances above a threshold. A transformed first-order
autoregressive process carries dependence through time. We extend this baseline
along two axes. The first is temporal. The autoregressive prior links each day
only to the one before, and we add a further lag to capture longer-range
dependence in the record. The second is spatial. The baseline assumes a
stationary \Matern covariance, which is restrictive over a continental network.
We therefore introduce the multi-resolution thin-plate spline basis of
\textcite{tzeng_resolution_2018} as fixed-effect covariates in the mean,
following the basis-function idea reviewed above, so as to relax stationarity at
little additional cost. Each axis is a candidate extension rather than a
replacement of the baseline, and together they are intended to improve prediction
of threshold exceedances.

The remainder of the thesis is organized as follows. \sref{sec:methodology}
reviews the spatial \skewt process of \textcite{morris_space-time_2017}, develops
the two extensions in turn (the standardized AR(2) prior on the latent dynamics
and the MRTS basis functions in the mean), and assembles them into a single
hierarchical model together with the MCMC algorithm used for inference.
\sref{sec:simulation} evaluates each extension on synthetic data, describing the
generating designs, the cross-validation scheme, and the held-out Brier
score, and reports the comparisons for the AR(2) and MRTS extensions separately.
\sref{sec:realdata} applies the models to the ozone monitoring data, comparing the
baseline against the two extensions in their ability to predict threshold
exceedances. \sref{sec:discussion} concludes and outlines directions for further
work. Technical derivations for the AR(2) prior are collected in the appendices.

\section{Methodology}\label{sec:methodology}

In this section, we first introduce the spatio-temporal skew-$t$ process (STP) proposed by \textcite{morris_space-time_2017}, and then develop two extensions of it. The first extends the autoregressive structure on the Gaussian-copula margins of the latent processes from AR(1) to AR(2). The second augments the regression component with multi-resolution thin-plate spline (MRTS) basis functions to capture non-stationary mean structure. Lastly, we present the full hierarchical model together with the MCMC algorithm used for inference.

\subsection{The spatio-temporal skew-$t$ model}\label{sec:sttskewt}

Let $Y_t(\bs)$ denote the response at spatial location $\bs \in \calD \subset \calR^2$ on day $t \in \{1, \ldots, n_t\}$.  Following \textcite{morris_space-time_2017}, we model
\begin{align}
  Y_t(\bs) \;=\; \bX_t(\bs)^\top \bbeta \;+\; \lambda\, \sigma_t(\bs)\, |z_t(\bs)| \;+\; \sigma_t(\bs)\, v_t(\bs), \label{eq:fullmodel}
\end{align}
where $\bX_t(\bs)$ is a $p$-vector of covariates, $\bbeta$ is the corresponding regression coefficient vector, $\lambda \in \calR$ is a scalar skewness parameter, and $v_t(\bs)$ is a standard Gaussian process with stationary isotropic \Matern correlation
\begin{align}
  \rho(h) \;=\; (1 - \gamma)\, I(h = 0) \;+\; \gamma\, \frac{1}{\Gamma(\nu)\, 2^{\nu - 1}} \left( \sqrt{2\nu}\, \frac{h}{\varphi} \right)^{\nu} K_\nu\!\left( \sqrt{2\nu}\, \frac{h}{\varphi} \right), \label{eq:matern}
\end{align}
where $\varphi > 0$ is the spatial range, $\nu > 0$ is the smoothness, $\gamma \in [0, 1]$ is the proportion of variance attributed to the spatial component, $K_\nu$ is the modified Bessel function of the second kind, and $h = \|\bs_1 - \bs_2\|$ is the Euclidean distance between sites $\bs_1$ and $\bs_2$.  Marginalizing over $|z_t(\bs)|$ and $\sigma_t(\bs)$, the finite-dimensional vector $\bY_t = [Y_t(\bs_1), \ldots, Y_t(\bs_n)]^\top$ follows an $n$-dimensional \skewt distribution; consequently \eref{eq:fullmodel} provides asymmetric and heavy-tailed margins together with asymptotic dependence between nearby sites.

\paragraph{Censoring.}
Because the EPA compliance criterion focuses on exceedances of a high level $L$, the model fits only the upper tail by censoring the data at a pre-specified threshold $T \le L$.  Writing the partially censored response $\widetilde{Y}_t(\bs) = \max\{Y_t(\bs), T\}$, inference is based on $\widetilde{\bY}_t = [\widetilde{Y}_t(\bs_1), \ldots, \widetilde{Y}_t(\bs_n)]^\top$, and the censored values below $T$ are imputed inside the MCMC.

\paragraph{Random Partition.}
To remove the long-range asymptotic dependence that arises when a single scalar $z$ and $\sigma^2$ are shared across the entire domain, the model uses a daily random partition.  For each day $t$, let $\bw_{t1}, \ldots, \bw_{tK}$ denote $K$ spatial knots in $\calD$, and define the partition subregions
\begin{align}
  P_{tk} \;=\; \{\bs \in \calD : k = \argmin_\ell \|\bs - \bw_{t\ell}\| \}, \qquad k = 1, \ldots, K.
\end{align}
For $\bs \in P_{tk}$ we set $z_t(\bs) = z_{tk}$ and $\sigma^2_t(\bs) = \sigma^2_{tk}$, with $z_{tk} \iid N(0, 1)$ and $\sigma^2_{tk} \iid \mathrm{IG}(a/2, b/2)$.  Within each subregion the model retains the \skewt form, while sites in different partitions are not asymptotically dependent.

\paragraph{Temporal Dependence.}
Daily temporal dependence is introduced on the latent parameters that drive the tail, namely the knot locations $\bw_{tk}$, the skewness latents $z_{tk}$, and the scale parameters $\sigma^2_{tk}$.  To preserve the marginal \skewt distribution at every day, each parameter is first transformed to a standard normal scale via the probability integral transform (PIT):
\begin{align}
  w^*_{tki} &= \Phi^{-1}\!\left[\frac{w_{tki} - \min(\bs_i)}{\max(\bs_i) - \min(\bs_i)}\right], \quad i = 1, 2, \label{eq:wstar}\\
  z^*_{tk}  &= \Phi^{-1}\{\mathrm{HN}(z_{tk})\}, \label{eq:zstar}\\
  \sigma^{2*}_{tk} &= \Phi^{-1}\{\mathrm{IG}(\sigma^2_{tk})\}, \label{eq:sigmastar}
\end{align}
where $\Phi$ is the standard normal distribution function, $\mathrm{HN}$ is the half-normal distribution function, and $\mathrm{IG}$ is the inverse-gamma distribution function with parameters $(a/2, b/2)$.  After transformation, each $\bw^*_{tk}, z^*_{tk}, \sigma^{2*}_{tk}$ has a standard normal marginal distribution, and \textcite{morris_space-time_2017} place a stationary AR(1) time series on each of them:
\begin{align}
  \bw^*_{tk} \mid \bw^*_{t-1, k} &\sim N_2\!\left(\phi_w\, \bw^*_{t-1, k},\; (1 - \phi_w^2)\, \bI_2\right), \label{eq:ar1-w}\\
  z^*_{tk} \mid z^*_{t-1, k} &\sim N\!\left(\phi_z\, z^*_{t-1, k},\; 1 - \phi_z^2\right), \label{eq:ar1-z}\\
  \sigma^{2*}_{tk} \mid \sigma^{2*}_{t-1, k} &\sim N\!\left(\phi_\sigma\, \sigma^{2*}_{t-1, k},\; 1 - \phi_\sigma^2\right), \label{eq:ar1-sigma}
\end{align}
with $|\phi_w|, |\phi_z|, |\phi_\sigma| < 1$, and initial values $\bw^*_{1k} \sim N_2(\bZero, \bI_2)$, $z^*_{1k} \sim N(0, 1)$, $\sigma^{2*}_{1k} \sim N(0, 1)$.  Transforming back through \eref{eq:wstar}--\eref{eq:sigmastar} gives $\bw_{tk} \sim \mathrm{Unif}(\calD)$, $z_{tk} \sim \mathrm{HN}(0, 1)$, and $\sigma^2_{tk} \sim \mathrm{IG}(a/2, b/2)$ at every $t$, so that the spatial \skewt structure of \eref{eq:fullmodel} is preserved at each day.

\subsection{Extremal dependence}\label{sec:extdep}

For exceedance prediction, what matters is how strongly the \skewt process couples extreme values at two sites, and how that coupling changes with their separation.
One measure of this extremal dependence is the $\chi$ statistic \parencite{Coles1999}.
For a stationary and isotropic spatial process, the $\chi$ statistic for two locations separated by distance $h$ is
\begin{align} \label{eq:chih}
  \chi(h) = \lim_{c \rightarrow c^*} \Pr[Y(\bs + h) > c \mid Y(\bs) > c],
\end{align}
where $c^*$ is the upper limit of the support of $Y$; for the \skewt distribution $c^* = \infty$.
If $\chi(h) = 0$, then observations are asymptotically independent at distance $h$.
For Gaussian processes, $\chi(h) = 0$ regardless of the distance $h$, so they are not suitable for modeling asymptotically dependent extremes.
Unlike the Gaussian process, the \skewt process is asymptotically dependent.
However, one problem with the spatial \skewt process is that $\lim_{h \rightarrow \infty} \chi(h) > 0$.
This occurs because all observations, both near and far, share the same $z$ and $\sigma$ terms.
Such long-range dependence is undesirable over large domains, where only local dependence is expected.
\textcite{morris_space-time_2017} avoid this problem through a random partitioning mechanism, described in detail in \sref{sec:sttskewt}.

\subsection{Extension to AR(2) time-varying coefficients}\label{sec:ar2}

In this section, we generalize the AR(1) prior \eref{eq:ar1-w}--\eref{eq:ar1-sigma} on the transformed latents to a stationary AR(2) prior.

\paragraph{The AR(2) prior.}
Writing $\{Y_t\}$ for any scalar component of $\bw^*_{tk}$, or for $z^*_{tk}$ or $\sigma^{2*}_{tk}$ at a fixed knot $k$, the prior is the stationary AR(2) recursion
\begin{align}
  Y_t \;=\; \phi_1\, Y_{t-1} \;+\; \phi_2\, Y_{t-2} \;+\; \epsilon_t,
  \qquad \epsilon_t \iid N(0, \sigma_\epsilon^2),
  \qquad t \ge 3, \label{eq:ar2-recursion}
\end{align}
with $(\phi_1, \phi_2)$ confined to the stationarity triangle
\begin{align}
  |\phi_2| < 1, \qquad \phi_1 + \phi_2 < 1, \qquad \phi_2 - \phi_1 < 1. \label{eq:stab-triangle}
\end{align}
Because the inverse PIT \eref{eq:wstar}--\eref{eq:sigmastar} returns the half-normal, inverse-gamma, and uniform target margins only when $\gamma_0 := \mathrm{Var}(Y_t) = 1$, the innovation variance is not free: solving the Yule--Walker equations under $\gamma_0 = 1$ (Appendix~\ref{app:ar2-moments}) fixes the standardized autocovariances
\begin{align}
  \gamma_1 \;=\; \frac{\phi_1}{1 - \phi_2}, \qquad
  \gamma_2 \;=\; \phi_1\, \gamma_1 + \phi_2, \label{eq:gamma12}
\end{align}
\begin{align}
  \sigma_\epsilon^2 \;=\; 1 - \phi_1\, \gamma_1 - \phi_2\, \gamma_2. \label{eq:sigeps}
\end{align}
Setting $\phi_2 = 0$ gives $\gamma_1 = \phi_1$ and $\sigma_\epsilon^2 = 1 - \phi_1^2$, the AR(1) form of \eref{eq:ar1-w}--\eref{eq:ar1-sigma}, so the AR(2) prior strictly nests the prior of \textcite{morris_space-time_2017}.

\paragraph{Stationary initial pair.}
The recursion \eref{eq:ar2-recursion} needs two seeds, and independent $N(0, 1)$ seeds would leave $\mathrm{Var}(Y_t) \ne 1$ over the first days and break the margin.  We therefore draw the initial pair from the stationary bivariate Gaussian
\begin{align}
  \begin{pmatrix} Y_1 \\ Y_2 \end{pmatrix}
  \;\sim\;
  N_2\!\left(
    \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix},\;
    \begin{pmatrix} 1 & \gamma_1 \\ \gamma_1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}
  \right), \label{eq:joint-init}
\end{align}
so that $\mathrm{Var}(Y_t) = 1$ holds from $t = 1$ onward.

\paragraph{Application to the three transformed parameters.}
Mirroring the AR(1) block \eref{eq:ar1-w}--\eref{eq:ar1-sigma}, the AR(2) prior \eref{eq:ar2-recursion} with initial condition \eref{eq:joint-init} replaces it component-wise:
\begin{align}
  \bw^*_{tk} \mid \bw^*_{t-1, k}, \bw^*_{t-2, k}
    &\sim N_2\!\left(\phi^{(w)}_1\, \bw^*_{t-1, k} + \phi^{(w)}_2\, \bw^*_{t-2, k},\; \sigma_{\epsilon, w}^2\, \bI_2\right), \label{eq:ar2-w}\\
  z^*_{tk} \mid z^*_{t-1, k}, z^*_{t-2, k}
    &\sim N\!\left(\phi^{(z)}_1\, z^*_{t-1, k} + \phi^{(z)}_2\, z^*_{t-2, k},\; \sigma_{\epsilon, z}^2\right), \label{eq:ar2-z}\\
  \sigma^{2*}_{tk} \mid \sigma^{2*}_{t-1, k}, \sigma^{2*}_{t-2, k}
    &\sim N\!\left(\phi^{(\sigma)}_1\, \sigma^{2*}_{t-1, k} + \phi^{(\sigma)}_2\, \sigma^{2*}_{t-2, k},\; \sigma_{\epsilon, \sigma}^2\right), \label{eq:ar2-sigma}
\end{align}
where each pair $(\phi^{(\cdot)}_1, \phi^{(\cdot)}_2)$ lies in the stationarity triangle \eref{eq:stab-triangle}, the innovation variances follow from \eref{eq:gamma12}--\eref{eq:sigeps}, and the initial pairs follow \eref{eq:joint-init} component-wise.  After the inverse PIT \eref{eq:wstar}--\eref{eq:sigmastar}, the back-transformed $\bw_{tk}, z_{tk}, \sigma^2_{tk}$ keep the same marginals as the AR(1) baseline, so the spatial \skewt model \eref{eq:fullmodel} is preserved at every $t$.  The Gibbs-within-MH full conditional that updates each $Y_t$ now carries three prior factors (self, lag-1, lag-2); its explicit form and the Metropolis--Hastings acceptance ratio are given in Appendix~\ref{app:full-cond}.

\subsection{Incorporating MRTS covariates}\label{sec:meth-mrts}

\textcite{tzeng_resolution_2018} proposed the multi-resolution thin-plate spline (MRTS) basis function, a basis whose functions are ordered from coarse to increasingly fine resolution. This ordering lets a model add spatial detail one resolution at a time; we define the basis below on a $2$-dimensional spatial domain. Consider $n$ distinct locations, $\bs_1, \cdots, \bs_n$, where $\bs_i = (x_{i1}, x_{i2})^\top \in \calR^2$ for $i = 1, \ldots, n$, and let
\begin{equation}
  \boldsymbol{X} \;=\;
  \begin{pmatrix}
    1 & x_{11} & x_{12} \\
    \vdots & \vdots & \vdots \\
    1 & x_{n1} & x_{n2}
  \end{pmatrix}
  \label{eq:mrts-X}
\end{equation}
. Then the MRTS basis functions $f_k(\bs)$ at a location $\bs = (x_1, x_2)^\top$ are defined as
\begin{equation}
  f_k(\bs) \;=\;
  \begin{cases}
    1, & k = 1, \\
    x_{k-1}, & k = 2, 3, \\
    \lambda_{k-3}^{-1} \big\{ \bphi(\bs) \,-\, \bPhi \boldsymbol{X}( \boldsymbol{X}^\top \boldsymbol{X} )^{-1} \, \boldsymbol{x} \big\}^\top \bv_{k-3}, & k = 4, \ldots, n,
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:mrts-basis}
\end{equation}
where $\boldsymbol{x} = (1, x_1, x_2)^\top$, $\bphi(\bs) = (\phi_1(\bs), \ldots, \phi_n(\bs))^\top$ with
\begin{equation}
  \phi_i(\bs) \;=\; \frac{1}{8\pi}\,\|\bs - \bs_i\|^2 \, \log\!\big(\|\bs - \bs_i\|\big), \qquad i = 1, \ldots, n,
  \label{eq:tps-kernel}
\end{equation}
and $\bPhi$ is the $n \times n$ matrix with $(i,j)$-th element $\phi_j(\bs_i)$, $\bv_k$ is the $k$-th column of $\bV$, and $\bV\,\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_n)\,\bV^\top$ is the eigen-decomposition of $\bQ\,\bPhi\,\bQ$ with $\lambda_1 \ge \cdots \ge \lambda_n$ and $\bQ = \bI - \boldsymbol{X}(\boldsymbol{X}^\top \boldsymbol{X})^{-1} \boldsymbol{X}^\top$.

The MRTS basis enters the STP through the regression component. Fix the number of basis functions $K_{\mathrm{MRTS}} \ge 1$ and let
\begin{align}
 \bff(\bs) \;=\; \big(f_1(\bs),\, f_2(\bs),\, \ldots,\, f_{K_{\mathrm{MRTS}}}(\bs)\big)^\top
 \label{eq:mrts-f}
\end{align}
collect the first $K_{\mathrm{MRTS}}$ basis functions defined in \eref{eq:mrts-basis}. Then the day-$t$ covariate vector $\bX_t(\bs)$ in \eref{eq:fullmodel} is replaced by
\begin{align}
 \bX^*_t(\bs)
 &= \big[\bff(\bs)^\top,\; \bX_t(\bs)^\top\big]^\top \notag\\
 &= \big[f_1(\bs),\; f_2(\bs),\, \ldots,\, f_{K_{\mathrm{MRTS}}}(\bs),\; \mathrm{X}_1(\bs, t),\, \ldots,\, \mathrm{X}_p(\bs, t)\big]^\top,
 \label{eq:mrts-augmented}
\end{align}
where $\mathrm{X}_j(\bs, t)$ represents the $j$-th exogenous covariate of $\bX_t(\bs)$. The model in \eref{eq:fullmodel} can then be expressed as
\begin{align}
  Y_t(\bs) \;=\; \bX^*_t(\bs)^\top \bbeta^* \;+\; \lambda\, \sigma_t(\bs)\, |z_t(\bs)| \;+\; \sigma_t(\bs)\, v_t(\bs),
  \label{eq:fullmodel-mrts}
\end{align}
which is identical to the STP model \eref{eq:fullmodel} on all components other than the mean, with $\bbeta^* \in \calR^{K_{\mathrm{MRTS}} + p}$.  Because the MRTS basis functions depend only on location, each site $\bs$ shares the same ordered set of basis values $\bff(\bs)$ at every day $t$.  The number of basis functions $K_{\mathrm{MRTS}}$ is the practical tuning parameter: \sref{sec:sim-design} compares $K_{\mathrm{MRTS}} \in \{0, 3, 5, 8, 10, 12, 15, 20, 25\}$ on simulated data of different models.

\subsection{Hierarchical model}\label{sec:meth-hier}

Following \textcite{morris_space-time_2017}, we write the spatial \skewt threshold-exceedance model as a Bayesian hierarchical model, fit by Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC).  The data are partially censored \skewt observations: at each MCMC iteration we impute the values below the threshold $T$, so that estimation proceeds on the completed $\bY$.  Conditional on the partition latents $z_{tk}(\bs)$, $\sigma^2_{tk}(\bs)$ and the subregions $P_{tk}$, $\bY$ is multivariate Gaussian.  The knot locations $\bw$ that define the partition are themselves unknown and random.  Their number of knots $K$, by contrast, we do not also make random, since that is computationally onerous; instead we fix it at several values and select among them by held-out Brier scores (\sref{sec:sim-scoring}).  The hierarchical model is
\begin{align}
  Y_t(\bs) \mid z_t(\bs), \sigma_t^2(\bs), P_{tk}, \Theta
    &= \bX_t(\bs)^\top \bbeta + \lambda\, \sigma_t(\bs)\, |z_t(\bs)| + \sigma_t(\bs)\, v_t(\bs), \label{eq:hier}\\
  z_t(\bs)        &= z_{tk}     \text{ if } \bs \in P_{tk}, \nonumber\\
  \sigma^2_t(\bs) &= \sigma^2_{tk} \text{ if } \bs \in P_{tk}, \nonumber\\
  \lambda         &\sim N(0, \sigma^2_\lambda), \nonumber\\
  v_t(\bs) \mid \Theta &\sim \Matern(\bZero, \bSigma), \nonumber
\end{align}
where $\Theta = \{\varphi, \nu, \gamma, \lambda, \bbeta\}$ and $\bSigma$ is the \Matern covariance of \eref{eq:matern}.  The priors on $\bw^*_{tk}$, $z^*_{tk}$ and $\sigma^{2*}_{tk}$ follow \eref{eq:ar1-w}--\eref{eq:ar1-sigma} for the baseline, or \eref{eq:ar2-w}--\eref{eq:ar2-sigma} under our AR(2) extension.  Equation~\eref{eq:hier} is the baseline of \textcite{morris_space-time_2017}; our contributions are the AR(2) priors above and the MRTS mean term $\bX^*_t(\bs)^\top \bbeta^*$ of \eref{eq:fullmodel-mrts}, which replace the latent dynamics and the mean while leaving the censoring layer and \Matern covariance unchanged.  Predictions at new sites use Bayesian kriging on the multivariate normal full conditional of $\bY_t$.

\section{Simulation study}\label{sec:simulation}

In this section, we present the results from a simulation study to investigate how the AR(2) time-varying structure on latent variables and the number of thin-plate spline basis functions impact the accuracy of predictions made by the model and to compare with Gaussian methods and methods without the proposed extensions.

\subsection{Design}\label{sec:sim-design}

All simulation designs generate data from the hierarchical model of \sref{sec:meth-hier} on $n_s = 144$ sites, drawn uniformly on $[0,10] \times [0,10]$, over $n_t = 50$ independent days.  In the data generation the regression mean is held constant, $\bX^\top \bbeta = 10$ (the MRTS experiment below adds a mean-zero non-stationary random effect to one design, which leaves the regression mean at $10$), while every fitted model includes an intercept and a first-order spatial trend.  For each design we simulate $10$ data sets, each split into $100$ training sites and $44$ withheld test sites, and run every MCMC chain for $20,000$ iterations with a burn-in period of $10,000$ iterations.  Throughout, $q(0.80)$ denotes the $80$th sample quantile of the data,   and the five baseline analysis models are
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=3em]
  \item Gaussian marginal, $K = 1$ knot;
  \item \Skewt marginal, $K = 1$ knot, no thresholding;
  \item Symmetric-$t$ marginal, $K = 1$ knot, threshold $T = q(0.80)$;
  \item \Skewt marginal, $K = 5$ knots, no thresholding;
  \item Symmetric-$t$ marginal, $K = 5$ knots, threshold $T = q(0.80)$.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Experiment for AR(2) extension.}\label{sec:sim-ar2-design}

This experiment embeds a fixed AR(2) mechanism into the \skewt generating process, so the AR(2) analysis model can be tested against data with a known autoregressive structure.  All four generating designs are \skewt with $\lambda = 3$, with the latent $\sigma^2$, $\bw$, and $z$ processes evolving through a fixed AR(2) recursion \eref{eq:ar2-w}--\eref{eq:ar2-sigma} whose coefficients are constant across time:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=3em]
  \item $K = 1$ knot, strong dependence $(\phi_1, \phi_2) = (0.80, -0.35)$;
  \item $K = 1$ knot, weak dependence $(\phi_1, \phi_2) = (0.12, -0.05)$;
  \item $K = 5$ knots, strong dependence $(\phi_1, \phi_2) = (0.80, -0.35)$;
  \item $K = 5$ knots, weak dependence $(\phi_1, \phi_2) = (0.12, -0.05)$.
\end{enumerate}
Both coefficient pairs lie inside the stationarity triangle \eref{eq:stab-triangle}.  Each data set is fit by nine analysis models: the five baseline models above, together with four temporal variants: \skewt models with AR(1) and AR(2) time-varying coefficients (following \eref{eq:ar2-w}--\eref{eq:ar2-sigma}) at $K = 1$ and $K = 5$ knots. 

\paragraph{Experiment for MRTS extension.}\label{sec:sim-mrts-design}

This experiment enriches the regression component with the MRTS basis functions $f_1(\bs), \ldots, f_{K_{\mathrm{MRTS}}}(\bs)$ of \sref{sec:meth-mrts}.  We use two generating designs:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \Skewt, $K = 5$ knots, $\lambda = 3$, with the constant mean $\bX^\top \bbeta = 10$;
  \item \Skewt, $K = 1$ knot, $\lambda = 3$, with an additive non-stationary spatial random effect $u_t(\bs)$ (specified below).
\end{enumerate}

The second design moves the non-stationarity into the dependence, to expose the structural limit of a fixed-effect mean basis.  It adds a mean-zero, per-day random effect built from the cosine basis, on $[0,10]^2$ domain:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Two fixed cosine fields with centres $\bc_1 = (0, 10)^\top$, $\bc_2 = (7.5, 2.5)^\top$ and native wavenumbers $\kappa_1 = \pi/10$, $\kappa_2 = \pi/5$,
    \begin{align}
      \phi_j(\bs) = \cos\!\big(\kappa_j \, \| \bs - \bc_j \|\big), \qquad j = 1, 2.
      \label{eq:nsdep-basis}
    \end{align}
  \item The random effect superposes them with independent, daily redrawn Gaussian weights,
    \begin{align}
      u_t(\bs) = \sum_{j=1}^{2} \xi_{tj}\, \phi_j(\bs), \qquad \xi_{tj} \iid N(0, \tau_j^2), \quad (\tau_1, \tau_2) = (5, 3),
      \label{eq:nsdep-re}
    \end{align}
    independently across $j$ and across days $t$.
\end{itemize}

The response adds $u_t$ to the 1-knot \skewt model,
\begin{align}
  Y_t(\bs) = 10 + \lambda \, \sigma_t(\bs) \, |z_t(\bs)| + \sigma_t(\bs) \, v_t(\bs) + u_t(\bs).
  \label{eq:nsdep-gen}
\end{align}

Marginalising over $\xi$ gives a zero-mean random effect whose pointwise variance $\sum_j \tau_j^2\, \phi_j(\bs)^2$ varies with location (Appendix~\ref{app:nsdep-cov}), so the dependence is non-stationary while the marginal mean stays constant at $10$.

Each of the five baseline models is fit across MRTS basis sizes $K_{\mathrm{MRTS}} \in \{0, 3, 5, 8, 10, 12, 15, 20, 25\}$, where $K_{\mathrm{MRTS}} = 0$ recovers the no-MRTS baseline.

\subsection{Cross validation}\label{sec:sim-scoring}

Models are compared using cross validation, with $100$ sites used as training sites to fit each setting and $44$ sites withheld for evaluating predictions on each replicate.  Because one of the primary goals of this model is to predict exceedances over a high level, we use Brier scores to compare the models \parencite{gneiting_strictly_2007}.  For an exceedance level $L$, the Brier score is
\begin{align}
  \mathrm{BS}(L) \;=\; \big\{\, I(y > L) \;-\; \widehat{P}(Y > L) \,\big\}^2,
  \label{eq:brier-score}
\end{align}
where $y$ is the held-out observation, $I(y > L)$ is the exceedance indicator, and $\widehat{P}(Y > L)$ is the posterior predictive probability of exceeding $L$.  We average the Brier scores over all test sites and days; lower values indicate better exceedance prediction.

In general, we find that the methods whose marginal matches the generating truth give the largest improvement over the Gaussian model, while a mismatched model can perform worse than Gaussian, particularly in the far tail (\fref{fig:bs-rel-ar2-fixed4}).  The preferred knot count tracks the data-generating truth rather than the strength of the temporal dependence: the one-knot models are best when the data are generated with one knot, and the five-knot models are best when the data are generated with five knots, with the AR(2) coefficients shifting the magnitude of the scores but not their ordering.

Adding AR(2) (or AR(1)) temporal structure does not help.  Even on data explicitly generated with an AR(2) recursion (the most favorable possible setting for the temporal extension), the temporal curves sit essentially on top of their non-temporal counterparts at every quantile and in every setting.  Predictive accuracy is governed by the flexibility of the marginal and the spatial partition, namely the \skewt margin and the number of knots, rather than by the autoregressive order of the latent coefficients.

The recovery of the AR(2) coefficients tells the same story (Table~\ref{tab:ar2-coef-recovery}).  Only the scale process is recovered with any fidelity, and only under strong dependence at a matched one-knot fit; the skewness process is recovered only partially and the knot-location process is essentially noise.  Recovery deteriorates as the knot count grows and as the dependence weakens, so even on AR(2)-generated data the temporal signal is only weakly identified.  In summary, the AR(2) structure should be treated as a candidate option rather than a default improvement.

\subsection{Results on MRTS comparison}\label{sec:sim-mrts-results}

In general, augmenting the regression component with MRTS basis functions does not deliver a stable improvement in upper-tail scores (\fref{fig:bs-mean-vs-K-mrts}).  As with the AR(2) comparison, the data-generating knot count dominates the ranking: the matching five-knot \skewt model is best on the five-knot setting, and the one-knot model is best on the random-effect process.

Enlarging the MRTS basis does not lower the mean Brier score on either setting.  On the five-knot setting every curve is essentially flat in $K_{\mathrm{MRTS}}$.  On the additive non-stationary spatial random-effect process the curves are likewise flat, with over-specified models destabilising at large $K_{\mathrm{MRTS}}$.  This second setting is the decisive one: the non-stationarity lives in the second moment (a daily-redrawn random effect $u_t$), whereas the MRTS basis enters only the fixed-effect mean.  Because $u_t$ has zero time-average, the time-pooled coefficient $\bbeta^*$ has no first-moment structure to recover, so the Brier- and recovery-versus-$K$ curves stay flat by construction, not for want of signal, since $u_t$ contributes substantial (spatially non-stationary) variance.  The MRTS mean term therefore yields no reliable tail-score gain: a fixed-effect mean cannot represent a non-stationary second-moment feature, and so the spatial mean is not the binding constraint on upper-tail prediction.

Overall, the simulation study indicates that both extensions enlarge the usable model class but that neither uniformly dominates the STP baseline.  The AR(2) prior provides temporal flexibility and the MRTS covariates enrich the spatial regression component, yet their empirical benefits depend on the data-generating mechanism and on the target exceedance level.  Whether the same pattern holds on real environmental data (and, if so, what it implies for practitioners choosing among the candidate settings) is the question taken up in the Data analysis section, where AR(2) and MRTS variants are treated as candidate options to be selected by held-out predictive scores rather than as default replacements for the AR(1) STP baseline.

\section{Real data analysis}\label{sec:realdata}

\textcite{morris_space-time_2017} introduced the spatio-temporal \skewt (STP) process to predict high-threshold ground-level ozone exceedances from U.S. EPA monitoring data.  We return to that same data here, asking not for a new model class but whether enlarging the STP candidate class along the two axes developed in this thesis, an AR(2) temporal prior and an MRTS spatial-mean term, yields better held-out tail prediction.  The comparison is therefore designed to identify the exceedance levels, if any, at which the enlarged candidate class improves on the STP baseline, rather than to select a single overall-best model.

The response is the daily maximum 8-hour ozone concentration for the $31$ days of July 2005 at United States Air Quality System (AQS) monitoring sites, with the co-located Community Multi-scale Air Quality (CMAQ) model output as the sole covariate.  Following \textcite{morris_space-time_2017}, the regression component is $\bX_t(\bs) = [1,\text{CMAQ}_t(\bs)]^\top$, so that each model acts as a statistical calibration of CMAQ against the monitor observations.  

\subsection{Model comparisons}

The baseline candidate set follows that of \textcite{morris_space-time_2017}.  We fit Gaussian and \skewt marginals over a range of partition sizes, $K \in \{1,5,6,7,8,9,10,15\}$, and censor the response at three levels to contrast no, moderate, and high censoring: no censoring ($T=0$), a moderate threshold ($T=50$ ppb, the $0.42$ sample quantile), and a high threshold ($T=75$ ppb, the $0.92$ sample quantile).  For settings censored at $T=75$ ppb, we use a symmetric-$t$ marginal, as in the simulation study.  Each setting is fit both with and without a latent time series.  

On top of this baseline we add the two extensions developed in this thesis.  The first replaces the AR(1) latent dynamics with the standardized AR(2) prior of \sref{sec:ar2}; the second augments the regression component with MRTS basis functions, so that non-stationary spatial mean structure can be absorbed by multi-resolution thin-plate splines. 

Each MCMC chain is run for $30{,}000$ iterations with a burn-in period of $25{,}000$ iterations. The comparison draws on $800$ monitoring sites and is evaluated by two-fold cross validation, with $400$ training and $400$ validation sites per fold, scored by the held-out Brier score of \sref{sec:sim-scoring}.

\subsection{Results}

Across the upper tail, the proposed extensions surface among the best models at scattered exceedance levels rather than improving prediction uniformly, so no single extension, and no single setting, is best across the whole tail (Table~\ref{tab:ozone-top2-brier}).  The extensions nonetheless earn their place by supplying the leading model at specific points along the tail that the baseline alone does not reach.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

This thesis asked not whether the spatio-temporal \skewt process predicts
high-threshold exceedances well, which \textcite{morris_space-time_2017} already
established, but where within its four components room for sharper
upper-tail prediction remains.  To probe it, we enlarged the model along two
opposing axes: an AR(2) latent prior adding temporal memory, and an MRTS mean term
adding a flexible non-stationary mean (a spatial trend).  We scored both on held-out data rather
than asserting either as a default.  The temporal axis yielded almost no usable
signal: even on AR(2)-generated data the temporal curves sat on top of their
non-temporal counterparts at every quantile, and the recovered coefficients
(Table~\ref{tab:ar2-coef-recovery}) were only weakly identified: recovery was
reliable mainly for the scale latent under strong dependence at matched low-$K$
settings, while recovery for the skewness and knot-location latents was
unstable, with large posterior standard deviations and attenuation toward zero,
especially as $K$ increased or dependence weakened.  Tail prediction is set by
how faithfully the marginal and the spatial partition capture the upper tail,
not by the memory of the latent coefficients.  The
spatial axis was more favorable but no more stable, with the MRTS basis improving
selected scores yet failing to hold across families or quantile levels; and while
the far-tail ranking is sensitive to the handful of sites exceeding the highest
thresholds, so that the single best setting shifts with the split, the broad
pattern in which the baseline, AR(2), and MRTS families recur among the top ranks
persists across all splits, and it is that persistence that justifies retaining
the extensions.

The lesson reaches past ozone, since the same target, a spatial probability
statement about the upper tail at unmonitored sites, arises for any heavy-tailed,
asymmetric environmental field regulated by a high threshold, from precipitation
extremes to heat warnings: temporal order and mean flexibility are not free
improvements to such a model, but neither are they idle.  Rather than replacing
the STP baseline, the standardized AR(2) prior and the MRTS mean enlarge it into
a candidate class from which held-out tail scores can select the setting that
fits the data at hand, giving practitioners who must predict high-threshold
exceedances a broader, data-driven choice than any single fixed model can offer.

\subsection{Stationary moments of the standardized AR(2)}\label{app:ar2-moments}

We derive the Yule--Walker moments \eref{eq:gamma12}--\eref{eq:sigeps} used to standardize the AR(2) prior \eref{eq:ar2-recursion} to unit marginal variance.  Let $\gamma_h = \mathrm{Cov}(Y_t, Y_{t-h})$ be the stationary autocovariances, normalized so that $\gamma_0 = \mathrm{Var}(Y_t) = 1$.  Multiplying the recursion \eref{eq:ar2-recursion} by $Y_{t-h}$ for $h \ge 1$ and taking expectations, and using that $\epsilon_t$ is independent of $Y_{t-h}$, yields the Yule--Walker recursion
\begin{align}
  \gamma_h \;=\; \phi_1\, \gamma_{h-1} \;+\; \phi_2\, \gamma_{h-2}, \qquad h \ge 1. \label{eq:yw}
\end{align}
Evaluating \eref{eq:yw} at $h = 1$ and using the symmetry $\gamma_{-1} = \gamma_1$ with $\gamma_0 = 1$ gives $\gamma_1 = \phi_1 + \phi_2\, \gamma_1$, hence $\gamma_1 = \phi_1 / (1 - \phi_2)$, and at $h = 2$ gives $\gamma_2 = \phi_1\, \gamma_1 + \phi_2$; together these are \eref{eq:gamma12}.  Multiplying \eref{eq:ar2-recursion} instead by $Y_t$ and taking expectations, with $\mathrm{E}[Y_t\, \epsilon_t] = \sigma_\epsilon^2$ because $Y_t$ carries the current innovation, gives $\gamma_0 = \phi_1\, \gamma_1 + \phi_2\, \gamma_2 + \sigma_\epsilon^2$, and solving for $\sigma_\epsilon^2$ under $\gamma_0 = 1$ recovers \eref{eq:sigeps}.  The same normalization fixes the initial pair: $(Y_1, Y_2)$ has unit variances and lag-1 covariance $\gamma_1$, which is the stationary bivariate Gaussian \eref{eq:joint-init}.

\subsection{AR(2) full conditional for the Gibbs-within-MH update}\label{app:full-cond}

The AR(2) Markov structure factorizes the joint prior of $(Y_1, \ldots, Y_{n_t})$ as
\begin{align}
  p(Y_1, \ldots, Y_{n_t})
  \;=\; p(Y_1, Y_2) \prod_{s = 3}^{n_t} p(Y_s \mid Y_{s-1}, Y_{s-2}), \label{eq:joint-prior}
\end{align}
with $p(Y_1, Y_2)$ given by \eref{eq:joint-init} and each conditional factor for $s \ge 3$ following \eref{eq:ar2-recursion}.  For an interior time point $3 \le t \le n_t - 2$, the value $Y_t$ enters \eref{eq:joint-prior} through three factors: the self factor $p(Y_t \mid Y_{t-1}, Y_{t-2})$, the lag-1 factor $p(Y_{t+1} \mid Y_t, Y_{t-1})$, and the lag-2 factor $p(Y_{t+2} \mid Y_{t+1}, Y_t)$.  The full conditional in the Gibbs-within-MH update of $Y_t$ is therefore
\begin{align}
  \pi(Y_t \mid Y_{-t}, \bY)
  \;\propto\;
  L(\bY \mid Y_t) \,\cdot\,
  \underbrace{p(Y_t \mid Y_{t-1}, Y_{t-2})}_{\text{self}}
  \,\cdot\,
  \underbrace{p(Y_{t+1} \mid Y_t, Y_{t-1})}_{\text{lag-1}}
  \,\cdot\,
  \underbrace{p(Y_{t+2} \mid Y_{t+1}, Y_t)}_{\text{lag-2}}, \label{eq:full-cond}
\end{align}
where $L(\bY \mid Y_t)$ is the spatial likelihood contributed by $Y_t$ through the partition and Gaussian process structure of \eref{eq:fullmodel}.  The lag-2 factor is absent for $t = n_t - 1$ and both lag-1 and lag-2 factors are absent for $t = n_t$.  Under a symmetric Gaussian random-walk proposal $Y_t^{(c)} \sim N(Y_t, s^2)$, the Metropolis--Hastings acceptance ratio is
\begin{align}
  R \;=\; \min\!\left(1,\; \frac{\pi(Y_t^{(c)} \mid Y_{-t}, \bY)}{\pi(Y_t \mid Y_{-t}, \bY)}\right), \label{eq:mh-ratio}
\end{align}
in which all three prior factors must appear; omitting the lag-2 factor distorts the stationary distribution of the chain away from the AR(2) joint and produces a lag-2 autocorrelation that does not match the Yule--Walker value in \eref{eq:gamma12}.

\subsection{Marginal covariance of the non-stationary-dependence design}\label{app:nsdep-cov}

The second MRTS design of \sref{sec:sim-mrts-design} superposes two fixed cosine fields \eref{eq:nsdep-basis} with daily-redrawn Gaussian weights $\xi_{tj} \iid N(0, \tau_j^2)$ \eref{eq:nsdep-re}.  Marginalising over the weights, $u_t(\bs) = \sum_{j} \xi_{tj}\, \phi_j(\bs)$ has zero mean, $\mathrm{E}[u_t(\bs)] = \sum_j \phi_j(\bs)\, \mathrm{E}[\xi_{tj}] = 0$, and, by the independence of $\xi_{t1}$ and $\xi_{t2}$, the low-rank covariance
\begin{align}
  \mathrm{Cov}\big(u_t(\bs), u_t(\bs')\big)
    \;=\; \sum_{j=1}^{2} \tau_j^2\, \phi_j(\bs)\, \phi_j(\bs').
  \label{eq:nsdep-cov}
\end{align}
The pointwise variance $\mathrm{Var}\big(u_t(\bs)\big) = \sum_{j} \tau_j^2\, \phi_j(\bs)^2$ depends on location, so the dependence is non-stationary while the marginal mean stays constant at $10$.
