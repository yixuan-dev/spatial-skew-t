\section*{摘要}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{摘要}

為提升美國環境保護署 (US EPA) 對地面臭氧高值的監測能力，\textcite{morris_space-time_2017} 提出以時空 \skewt 過程為基礎的階層式貝氏模型，利用 AQS 監測站資料校正 CMAQ 化學傳輸模式在尾端的預測偏差。該模型結合 \Matern 協方差結構、空間隨機分割、上尾門檻檢查，以及 AR(1) 形式的時變參數，同時刻劃空間非定態性與時間相依性。本論文在此基礎上提出兩項延伸：其一，將時變參數的 AR(1) 先驗推廣為穩態 AR(2) 先驗，並透過 Yule--Walker 關係維持單位邊際變異數，使模型在保有原 \skewt 邊際結構的條件下捕捉更長距離的時間相依；其二，將多重解析度薄板樣條 (MRTS) 基底函數納入迴歸成分，以彌補定態 \Matern 協方差難以表現的非定態空間結構。模擬研究與美國臭氧資料分析皆顯示，所提兩項延伸在部分上尾分位下可獲得較原 Morris 模型更低之 Brier 分數，惟其優勢非全面性，而視資料生成機制及目標尾端水準而定。本文因此建議將 AR(2) 與 MRTS 兩種設定納入候選模型集合，並以樣本外預測分數作為最終選模依據。

\textbf{關鍵字}：時空 \skewt 過程、AR(2) 時變參數、多重解析度薄板樣條、隨機分割、階層式貝氏模型、臭氧預測

\section*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Abstract}

To improve the calibration of Community Multiscale Air Quality (CMAQ) model output by ground-level ozone monitoring data, \textcite{morris_space-time_2017} developed a hierarchical Bayesian framework based on a spatio-temporal \skewt process, combining a \Matern covariance, a random spatial partition, upper-tail censoring, and AR(1) time-varying coefficients to characterize non-stationary spatial dependence and temporal persistence.  This thesis extends that framework in two directions.  First, the AR(1) prior on the time-varying latent parameters is generalized to a stationary AR(2) prior, with Yule--Walker constraints used to preserve unit marginal variance and hence the spatial \skewt marginal structure at every time point.  Second, multi-resolution thin-plate spline (MRTS) basis functions are incorporated into the regression component to capture non-stationary spatial structure that the stationary \Matern covariance cannot represent.  Simulation studies and an analysis of U.S.\ ozone observations show that both extensions can achieve lower Brier scores than the original Morris model at selected upper-tail exceedance levels, although neither extension dominates uniformly across data-generating mechanisms or target quantiles.  The proposed extensions therefore enlarge the candidate model class and should be selected among using held-out predictive scores rather than treated as default replacements for the AR(1) baseline.

\textbf{Keywords}: spatio-temporal \skewt process; AR(2) time-varying coefficients; multi-resolution thin-plate splines; random partition; hierarchical Bayesian modeling; ozone prediction

\section{Introduction}

\section{Methodology}
\subsection{Spatio-temporal skew-t process}\label{sec:sttskewt}

The methodology in this thesis builds on the spatio-temporal \skewt model of \textcite{morris_space-time_2017}.  We briefly summarize that model here so that the AR(2) and MRTS extensions in the next two subsections can be stated against a common notation.

Let $Y_t(\bs)$ denote the response at spatial location $\bs \in \calD \subset \calR^2$ on day $t \in \{1, \ldots, n_t\}$.  Following \textcite{morris_space-time_2017}, we model
\begin{align}
  Y_t(\bs) \;=\; \bX_t(\bs)^\top \bbeta \;+\; \lambda\, \sigma_t(\bs)\, |z_t(\bs)| \;+\; \sigma_t(\bs)\, v_t(\bs), \label{eq:fullmodel}
\end{align}
where $\bX_t(\bs)$ is a $p$-vector of covariates, $\bbeta$ is the corresponding regression coefficient, $\lambda \in \calR$ is a scalar skewness parameter, and $v_t(\bs)$ is a standard Gaussian process with stationary isotropic \Matern correlation
\begin{align}
  \rho(h) \;=\; (1 - \gamma)\, I(h = 0) \;+\; \gamma\, \frac{1}{\Gamma(\nu)\, 2^{\nu - 1}} \left( \sqrt{2\nu}\, \frac{h}{\varphi} \right)^{\nu} K_\nu\!\left( \sqrt{2\nu}\, \frac{h}{\varphi} \right),
\end{align}
where $\varphi > 0$ is the spatial range, $\nu > 0$ is the smoothness, $\gamma \in [0, 1]$ is the proportion of variance attributed to the spatial component, $K_\nu$ is the modified Bessel function of the second kind, and $h = \|\bs_1 - \bs_2\|$.  Marginalizing over $|z_t(\bs)|$ and $\sigma_t(\bs)$, the finite-dimensional vector $\bY_t = [Y_t(\bs_1), \ldots, Y_t(\bs_n)]^\top$ follows an $n$-dimensional \skewt distribution; consequently \eref{eq:fullmodel} provides asymmetric and heavy-tailed margins together with asymptotic dependence between nearby sites.

To remove the long-range asymptotic dependence that arises when a single scalar $z$ and $\sigma^2$ are shared across the entire domain, the model uses a daily random partition.  For each day $t$, let $\bw_{t1}, \ldots, \bw_{tK}$ denote $K$ spatial knots in $\calD$, and define the partition subregions
\begin{align}
  P_{tk} \;=\; \{\bs \in \calD : k = \argmin_\ell \|\bs - \bw_{t\ell}\| \}, \qquad k = 1, \ldots, K.
\end{align}
For $\bs \in P_{tk}$ we set $z_t(\bs) = z_{tk}$ and $\sigma^2_t(\bs) = \sigma^2_{tk}$, with $z_{tk} \iid N(0, 1)$ and $\sigma^2_{tk} \iid \mathrm{IG}(a/2, b/2)$.  Within each subregion the model retains the \skewt form, while sites in different partitions are not asymptotically dependent.

Because the EPA compliance criterion focuses on exceedances of a high level $L$, the model fits only the upper tail by censoring the data at a pre-specified threshold $T \le L$.  Writing the partially censored response $\widetilde{Y}_t(\bs) = \max\{Y_t(\bs), T\}$, inference is based on $\widetilde{\bY}_t = [\widetilde{Y}_t(\bs_1), \ldots, \widetilde{Y}_t(\bs_n)]^\top$, and the censored values below $T$ are imputed inside the MCMC.

Daily temporal dependence is introduced on the latent parameters that drive the tail, namely the knot locations $\bw_{tk}$, the skewness latents $z_{tk}$, and the scale parameters $\sigma^2_{tk}$.  To preserve the marginal \skewt distribution at every day, each parameter is first transformed to a standard normal scale via the probability integral transform:
\begin{align}
  w^*_{tki} &= \Phi^{-1}\!\left[\frac{w_{tki} - \min(\bs_i)}{\max(\bs_i) - \min(\bs_i)}\right], \quad i = 1, 2, \label{eq:wstar}\\
  z^*_{tk}  &= \Phi^{-1}\{\mathrm{HN}(z_{tk})\}, \label{eq:zstar}\\
  \sigma^{2*}_{tk} &= \Phi^{-1}\{\mathrm{IG}(\sigma^2_{tk})\}, \label{eq:sigmastar}
\end{align}
where $\Phi$ is the standard normal distribution function, $\mathrm{HN}$ is the half-normal distribution function, and $\mathrm{IG}$ is the inverse-gamma distribution function with parameters $(a/2, b/2)$.  After transformation, each $\bw^*_{tk}, z^*_{tk}, \sigma^{2*}_{tk}$ has a standard normal marginal distribution, and \textcite{morris_space-time_2017} place a stationary AR(1) Gaussian time series on each of them:
\begin{align}
  \bw^*_{tk} \mid \bw^*_{t-1, k} &\sim N_2\!\left(\phi_w\, \bw^*_{t-1, k},\; (1 - \phi_w^2)\, \bI_2\right), \label{eq:ar1-w}\\
  z^*_{tk} \mid z^*_{t-1, k} &\sim N\!\left(\phi_z\, z^*_{t-1, k},\; 1 - \phi_z^2\right), \label{eq:ar1-z}\\
  \sigma^{2*}_{tk} \mid \sigma^{2*}_{t-1, k} &\sim N\!\left(\phi_\sigma\, \sigma^{2*}_{t-1, k},\; 1 - \phi_\sigma^2\right), \label{eq:ar1-sigma}
\end{align}
with $|\phi_w|, |\phi_z|, |\phi_\sigma| < 1$, and initial values $\bw^*_{1k} \sim N_2(\bZero, \bI_2)$, $z^*_{1k} \sim N(0, 1)$, $\sigma^{2*}_{1k} \sim N(0, 1)$.  Transforming back through \eref{eq:wstar}--\eref{eq:sigmastar} gives $\bw_{tk} \sim \mathrm{Unif}(\calD)$, $z_{tk} \sim \mathrm{HN}(0, 1)$, and $\sigma^2_{tk} \sim \mathrm{IG}(a/2, b/2)$ at every $t$, so that the spatial \skewt structure of \eref{eq:fullmodel} is preserved at each day.  The complete hierarchical model is fitted via MCMC with Gibbs and Metropolis--Hastings steps, with predictions at unobserved sites obtained by Bayesian kriging on the multivariate normal full conditional of $\bY_t$.

This \skewt baseline -- partitioned, censored, and AR(1)-correlated in time -- defines the reference specification against which the two proposed extensions of this thesis are evaluated.

\subsection{Extension to AR(2) time-varying coefficients}\label{sec:ar2}

The AR(1) time series in \eref{eq:ar1-w}--\eref{eq:ar1-sigma} links each transformed parameter on day $t$ to its value on day $t - 1$ only.  This may be too restrictive for daily atmospheric variables such as ozone, where persistence and weather-driven cycles can carry information across multiple lags.  We therefore replace the AR(1) prior on each transformed parameter by a stationary AR(2) prior, while preserving the unit marginal variance required by the probability integral transforms in \eref{eq:wstar}--\eref{eq:sigmastar}.

\paragraph{The AR(2) recursion.}
For a generic transformed parameter $\{Y_t\}_{t=1}^{n_t}$ -- standing in for any of the scalar components of $\bw^*_{tk}$, or for $z^*_{tk}$ or $\sigma^{2*}_{tk}$ at a fixed knot $k$ -- the AR(2) prior is
\begin{align}
  Y_t \;=\; \phi_1\, Y_{t-1} \;+\; \phi_2\, Y_{t-2} \;+\; \epsilon_t,
  \qquad \epsilon_t \iid N(0, \sigma_\epsilon^2),
  \qquad t \ge 3. \label{eq:ar2-recursion}
\end{align}
For the recursion to define a stationary process, $(\phi_1, \phi_2)$ must lie inside the stationarity triangle
\begin{align}
  |\phi_2| < 1, \qquad \phi_1 + \phi_2 < 1, \qquad \phi_2 - \phi_1 < 1. \label{eq:stab-triangle}
\end{align}
Just as in the AR(1) case, the AR(2) parameters $(\phi^{(w)}_1, \phi^{(w)}_2)$, $(\phi^{(z)}_1, \phi^{(z)}_2)$, and $(\phi^{(\sigma)}_1, \phi^{(\sigma)}_2)$ are separately defined for $\bw^*_{tk}$, $z^*_{tk}$, and $\sigma^{2*}_{tk}$.

\paragraph{Yule--Walker moments under unit marginal variance.}
We require $\gamma_0 := \mathrm{Var}(Y_t) = 1$ so that the inverse PIT applied to $Y_t$ recovers the half-normal, inverse-gamma, or uniform target margins on the original scale.  Multiplying \eref{eq:ar2-recursion} by $Y_{t-h}$ and taking expectations yields the Yule--Walker recursion
\begin{align}
  \gamma_h \;=\; \phi_1\, \gamma_{h-1} \;+\; \phi_2\, \gamma_{h-2}. \label{eq:yw}
\end{align}
Setting $h = 1$ and using the symmetry $\gamma_{-1} = \gamma_1$ gives
\begin{align}
  \gamma_1 \;=\; \frac{\phi_1}{1 - \phi_2}, \qquad
  \gamma_2 \;=\; \phi_1\, \gamma_1 + \phi_2, \label{eq:gamma12}
\end{align}
and multiplying \eref{eq:ar2-recursion} by $Y_t$ pins down the innovation variance
\begin{align}
  \sigma_\epsilon^2 \;=\; 1 - \phi_1\, \gamma_1 - \phi_2\, \gamma_2. \label{eq:sigeps}
\end{align}
Setting $\phi_2 = 0$ in \eref{eq:gamma12}--\eref{eq:sigeps} recovers the AR(1) form $\gamma_1 = \phi_1$, $\sigma_\epsilon^2 = 1 - \phi_1^2$, so the AR(2) prior strictly contains the AR(1) prior used by \textcite{morris_space-time_2017}.

\paragraph{Stationary joint of the initial pair.}
Because the recursion \eref{eq:ar2-recursion} requires two seeds, the joint distribution of $(Y_1, Y_2)$ has to be specified separately.  Independent $N(0, 1)$ initial values would create a transient regime in which $\mathrm{Var}(Y_t) \ne 1$ for the first several days, breaking the PIT mechanism that produces the correct \skewt margin.  Stationarity therefore forces the bivariate Gaussian initial condition
\begin{align}
  \begin{pmatrix} Y_1 \\ Y_2 \end{pmatrix}
  \;\sim\;
  N_2\!\left(
    \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix},\;
    \begin{pmatrix} 1 & \gamma_1 \\ \gamma_1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}
  \right), \label{eq:joint-init}
\end{align}
which yields the conditional density used when updating $Y_2$ alone:
\begin{align}
  Y_2 \mid Y_1 \;\sim\; N\!\left(\gamma_1\, Y_1,\; 1 - \gamma_1^2\right). \label{eq:p2-stationary}
\end{align}
Note that the mean of \eref{eq:p2-stationary} is $\gamma_1 Y_1$, not $\phi_1 Y_1$, and its variance is $1 - \gamma_1^2$, not $\sigma_\epsilon^2$; the two expressions coincide only when $\phi_2 = 0$.  In the AR(2) implementation it is essential that both the simulator and the MCMC density at $t = 2$ use \eref{eq:p2-stationary} so that the marginal property $\mathrm{Var}(Y_t) = 1$ holds at every $t$.

\paragraph{Full conditional with three prior factors.}
The AR(2) Markov structure factorizes the joint prior of $(Y_1, \ldots, Y_{n_t})$ as
\begin{align}
  p(Y_1, \ldots, Y_{n_t})
  \;=\; p(Y_1, Y_2) \prod_{s = 3}^{n_t} p(Y_s \mid Y_{s-1}, Y_{s-2}), \label{eq:joint-prior}
\end{align}
with $p(Y_1, Y_2)$ given by \eref{eq:joint-init} and each conditional factor for $s \ge 3$ following \eref{eq:ar2-recursion}.  For an interior time point $3 \le t \le n_t - 2$, the value $Y_t$ enters \eref{eq:joint-prior} through three factors: the self factor $p(Y_t \mid Y_{t-1}, Y_{t-2})$, the lag-1 factor $p(Y_{t+1} \mid Y_t, Y_{t-1})$, and the lag-2 factor $p(Y_{t+2} \mid Y_{t+1}, Y_t)$.  The full conditional in the Gibbs-within-MH update of $Y_t$ is therefore
\begin{align}
  \pi(Y_t \mid Y_{-t}, \bY)
  \;\propto\;
  L(\bY \mid Y_t) \,\cdot\,
  \underbrace{p(Y_t \mid Y_{t-1}, Y_{t-2})}_{\text{self}}
  \,\cdot\,
  \underbrace{p(Y_{t+1} \mid Y_t, Y_{t-1})}_{\text{lag-1}}
  \,\cdot\,
  \underbrace{p(Y_{t+2} \mid Y_{t+1}, Y_t)}_{\text{lag-2}}, \label{eq:full-cond}
\end{align}
where $L(\bY \mid Y_t)$ is the spatial likelihood contributed by $Y_t$ through the partition and Gaussian process structure of \eref{eq:fullmodel}.  The lag-2 factor is absent for $t = n_t - 1$ and both lag-1 and lag-2 factors are absent for $t = n_t$.  Under a symmetric Gaussian random-walk proposal $Y_t^{(c)} \sim N(Y_t, s^2)$, the Metropolis--Hastings acceptance ratio is
\begin{align}
  R \;=\; \min\!\left(1,\; \frac{\pi(Y_t^{(c)} \mid Y_{-t}, \bY)}{\pi(Y_t \mid Y_{-t}, \bY)}\right), \label{eq:mh-ratio}
\end{align}
in which all three prior factors must appear; omitting the lag-2 factor distorts the stationary distribution of the chain away from the AR(2) joint and produces a lag-2 autocorrelation that does not match the Yule--Walker value in \eref{eq:gamma12}.

\paragraph{Application to all three transformed parameters.}
The AR(2) prior \eref{eq:ar2-recursion} together with the stationary initial condition \eref{eq:joint-init} replaces \eref{eq:ar1-w}--\eref{eq:ar1-sigma} for each transformed parameter:
\begin{align}
  \bw^*_{tk} \mid \bw^*_{t-1, k}, \bw^*_{t-2, k}
    &\sim N_2\!\left(\phi^{(w)}_1\, \bw^*_{t-1, k} + \phi^{(w)}_2\, \bw^*_{t-2, k},\; \sigma_{\epsilon, w}^2\, \bI_2\right), \label{eq:ar2-w}\\
  z^*_{tk} \mid z^*_{t-1, k}, z^*_{t-2, k}
    &\sim N\!\left(\phi^{(z)}_1\, z^*_{t-1, k} + \phi^{(z)}_2\, z^*_{t-2, k},\; \sigma_{\epsilon, z}^2\right), \label{eq:ar2-z}\\
  \sigma^{2*}_{tk} \mid \sigma^{2*}_{t-1, k}, \sigma^{2*}_{t-2, k}
    &\sim N\!\left(\phi^{(\sigma)}_1\, \sigma^{2*}_{t-1, k} + \phi^{(\sigma)}_2\, \sigma^{2*}_{t-2, k},\; \sigma_{\epsilon, \sigma}^2\right), \label{eq:ar2-sigma}
\end{align}
where each pair $(\phi^{(\cdot)}_1, \phi^{(\cdot)}_2)$ lies inside the stationarity triangle \eref{eq:stab-triangle}, the innovation variances are obtained from \eref{eq:gamma12}--\eref{eq:sigeps}, and the initial pairs $(\bw^*_{1k}, \bw^*_{2k})$, $(z^*_{1k}, z^*_{2k})$, $(\sigma^{2*}_{1k}, \sigma^{2*}_{2k})$ follow the bivariate Gaussian \eref{eq:joint-init} component-wise.  After inverse transformation through \eref{eq:wstar}--\eref{eq:sigmastar}, the back-transformed parameters $\bw_{tk}, z_{tk}, \sigma^2_{tk}$ retain the same marginal distributions as in the AR(1) baseline, so that the spatial \skewt model \eref{eq:fullmodel} is preserved at every $t$.  Setting $\phi^{(\cdot)}_2 = 0$ in \eref{eq:ar2-w}--\eref{eq:ar2-sigma} recovers the Morris AR(1) specification, so the AR(2) extension nests the original time-series model and adds one additional autoregressive coefficient per latent process.

\section{Simulation study}
\subsection{Design}

The simulation study is used to evaluate whether the proposed extensions improve predictive performance beyond the space-time \skewt threshold model of \textcite{morris_space-time_2017}.  The benchmark simulation follows the structure of Morris et al.: observations are generated at $n_s=144$ spatial sites, sampled uniformly on $[0,10]\times[0,10]$, over $n_t=50$ independent time points.  The mean structure is constant in the data generation, $\bX^\top\bbeta=10$, while the fitted models include an intercept and first-order spatial trend.  The main data-generating settings include Gaussian data, \skewt data with one knot, \skewt data with five knots, a max-stable setting, and a Brown-Resnick max-stable setting.  For each setting, $50$ simulated data sets are generated.

For the baseline comparison, five Bayesian models are considered:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=3em]
  \item Gaussian marginal distribution with $K=1$ knot;
  \item \Skewt marginal distribution with $K=1$ knot and no thresholding;
  \item Symmetric-$t$ marginal distribution with $K=1$ knot and threshold $T=q(0.80)$;
  \item \Skewt marginal distribution with $K=5$ knots and no thresholding;
  \item Symmetric-$t$ marginal distribution with $K=5$ knots and threshold $T=q(0.80)$.
\end{enumerate}
Here $q(0.80)$ denotes the empirical $80$th percentile of the simulated response.  These five models represent the main combinations of tail behavior, thresholding, and spatial partitioning used in the Morris model.

Two additional simulation experiments are conducted for the extensions in this thesis.  First, to evaluate the AR(2) time-varying coefficient structure, data are generated with temporal dependence in the latent $\tau$, $\bw$, and $\bz$ processes.  The experiment uses $n=100$ sites and $T=30$ time points, with $90$ sites used for training and $10$ sites withheld for prediction.  We repeat the experiment $20$ times and compare three temporal structures: no time series, AR(1), and AR(2).  Each fitted model uses a \skewt marginal distribution with $K=5$ knots and no thresholding, and the MCMC is run for $5000$ iterations with $2500$ burn-in iterations.

A complementary AR(2) experiment embeds the temporal mechanism directly into the benchmark simulation, so that the AR(2) analysis model can be tested against data with a known autoregressive structure.  Two further data-generating settings are added.  Both produce one-knot \skewt data ($K=1$, $\lambda=3$) on the same $n_s=144$ sites and $n_t=50$ time points as the benchmark, but the latent $\tau$, $\bw$, and $z$ processes now evolve through a fixed AR(2) recursion \eref{eq:ar2-w}--\eref{eq:ar2-sigma} whose coefficients are held constant across time.  Setting~9 uses a strongly dependent coefficient pair $(\phi_1, \phi_2) = (0.80, -0.35)$, and Setting~10 a weakly dependent pair $(\phi_1, \phi_2) = (0.12, -0.05)$; both pairs lie inside the stationarity triangle \eref{eq:stab-triangle}.  For each setting, $50$ data sets are simulated and split into $100$ training and $44$ validation sites.  Each data set is fit by seven analysis methods: the five baseline models listed above, together with two AR(2)-augmented \skewt models---one with $K=1$ knot and one with $K=5$ knots---whose time-varying coefficients follow the AR(2) prior \eref{eq:ar2-w}--\eref{eq:ar2-sigma}.  Because the data are themselves generated with AR(2) dependence, this comparison gives the temporal extension its most favorable test.

Second, to evaluate the MRTS covariate extension, we augment the original five models with multi-resolution thin-plate spline basis functions \parencite{tzeng_resolution_2018}.  These basis functions are added to the regression component in order to capture nonstationary spatial structure that may not be fully represented by a stationary \Matern covariance.  We focus on simulation settings 4 and 5: setting 4 is a one-knot \skewt data-generating process with $\lambda=3$, and setting 5 is a five-knot partitioned \skewt data-generating process with $\lambda=3$.  For setting 4, each original model is compared with versions using $10$, $15$, and $20$ MRTS covariates.  For setting 5, each original model is compared with a version using $15$ MRTS covariates.  Each setting uses $50$ simulated data sets, and each MCMC chain is run for $20000$ iterations with $10000$ burn-in iterations.

\subsection{Cross validation}

Predictive performance is evaluated by withholding spatial sites from model fitting and scoring posterior predictive distributions at the held-out sites.  In the benchmark simulation, $100$ sites are used as training sites and $44$ sites are withheld for validation.  In the AR(2) experiment, the split is $90$ training sites and $10$ testing sites.  The MRTS experiment follows the benchmark simulation structure and compares each MRTS-augmented model with the corresponding non-MRTS model on the same simulated data sets.

Because the main inferential target is prediction of high-level exceedances, the primary scoring rule is the Brier score \parencite{gneiting_strictly_2007}.  For an exceedance level $L$, the Brier score is $\text{BS}(L) = \{ I(y > L) - \widehat{P}(Y > L) \}^2$, where $y$ is the held-out observation and $\widehat{P}(Y>L)$ is the posterior predictive probability of exceeding $L$.  The score is averaged over all held-out sites and time points, and lower values indicate better exceedance prediction.

For the extension experiments, we also record MAPE and quantile scores.  The quantile score at level $\alpha$ is used to evaluate the accuracy of posterior predictive quantiles, with lower values again indicating better prediction.  The reported exceedance and quantile levels are concentrated in the upper tail: the AR(2) experiment uses $q(0.90)$, $q(0.95)$, $q(0.98)$, and $q(0.99)$, while the MRTS experiment uses levels from $q(0.90)$ to $q(0.995)$.

\subsection{Results}

The benchmark simulation supports the main conclusion of \textcite{morris_space-time_2017}: when the fitted model matches the data-generating mechanism, it tends to improve predictive performance, and when the data are non-Gaussian the \skewt models generally improve over a Gaussian model.  Thresholded models are not uniformly better in the simulation setting, which is expected because most simulated data are generated directly from the full \skewt model rather than from a censored tail model.  The simulation therefore confirms that the \skewt framework provides flexibility for asymmetric and heavy-tailed data while remaining competitive with Gaussian methods when the data are light-tailed and symmetric.

A value below the dashed reference line at $1$ means the method predicts exceedances better than the Gaussian baseline at that quantile, and a value above it means worse.

Under strong serial dependence (Setting~9, Figure~\ref{fig:bs-rel-set9}), every \skewt and symmetric-$t$ method except the five-knot thresholded $t$-model improves on the Gaussian baseline, with relative Brier scores between roughly $0.94$ and $0.98$.  The one-knot \skewt model, which matches the $K=1$ data-generating truth, is the best method, with a relative score near $0.95$.  Critically, adding the AR(2) temporal structure does not help: the one-knot AR(2) \skewt curve sits essentially on top of---and slightly above---the one-knot non-temporal \skewt curve, and the five-knot AR(2) model tracks its non-temporal counterpart.  The five-knot thresholded $t$-model is the only method worse than Gaussian, and its relative score degrades sharply at the $0.99$ quantile.

Under weak serial dependence (Setting~10, Figure~\ref{fig:bs-rel-set10}) the pattern shifts.  The five-knot \skewt and five-knot thresholded $t$ models are now clearly best, with relative Brier scores mostly between about $0.88$ and $0.94$, while the one-knot models---including the one-knot AR(2) model---are essentially indistinguishable from the Gaussian baseline.  Once again the AR(2) and non-temporal curves nearly coincide at each knot count.  Far-tail estimates are noisy: at the $0.995$ quantile several methods rise above $1$ because few observations exceed such extreme thresholds.

Two messages emerge.  First, on data that are explicitly generated with AR(2) dependence---the most favorable possible setting for the temporal extension---fitting an AR(2) analysis model yields no measurable Brier-score gain over the corresponding non-temporal \skewt model at any quantile.  Predictive accuracy is governed by the marginal and spatial flexibility of the model, namely the \skewt margin and the number of knots, rather than by the autoregressive order of the latent coefficients.  Second, the preferred knot count depends on the dependence regime: strong temporal dependence favors the parsimonious one-knot model that matches the generating truth, whereas weak dependence favors the richer five-knot partition.  This reinforces the conclusion that the AR(2) structure should be treated as a candidate option rather than a default improvement.

A complementary set of missing-at-location experiments places the AR(2), AR(1), and no-time-series specifications against a range of dependence regimes; the resulting Brier scores at the $0.90$, $0.95$, and $0.99$ exceedance levels are collected in Table~\ref{tab:sim-ar2-missing-location}.  AR(2) improves mid-tail Brier scores in some scenarios -- most visibly when one or more of the latent autoregressive coefficients is large -- but AR(1) or the no-time-series baseline remains preferable in other scenarios or at specific tail levels, and the gains over AR(1) are typically smaller than the gap between any time-series model and the no-time-series baseline in runtime.  Temporal flexibility therefore does not translate into uniform predictive dominance.

\section{Ozone data analysis}

The real-data analysis applies the model comparison framework to daily maximum 8-hour ozone observations from July 2005.  The response is the AQS monitor observation, and the main covariate is the corresponding CMAQ model output.  Following the ozone analysis in \textcite{morris_space-time_2017}, the regression component uses $\bX_t(\bs) = [1,\text{CMAQ}_t(\bs)]^\top$, so the model is interpreted as a statistical calibration of CMAQ using site-level observations.  The full comparison data set contains $800$ monitoring sites observed over $31$ days.  We use two-fold cross validation, with $400$ sites used for training and $400$ sites used for validation in each fold.

The purpose of this analysis is not only to reproduce the Morris ozone comparison, but also to evaluate whether the AR(2) time-varying coefficient extension and the MRTS covariate extension are useful for real ozone prediction.  A preliminary MERRA-2 analysis with $100$ sites and $100$ time points showed that AR(2), AR(1), and no-time-series models have very similar MAPE, Brier scores, and quantile scores.  The ozone analysis therefore focuses on whether the enlarged model class can improve held-out tail prediction rather than assuming that the more flexible extension must be better.

\subsection{Model comparisons}

The candidate set contains approximately one hundred model settings.  The baseline settings include Gaussian, \skewt, symmetric-$t$, and max-stable models, with different numbers of knots, censoring thresholds, and time-series choices.  For the \skewt and symmetric-$t$ models, the number of knots includes values such as $K=1,5,6,7,8,9,10$, and $15$.  Censoring thresholds include no censoring, a moderate threshold such as $T=50$, and a high threshold such as $T=75$ ppb.  The threshold $75$ ppb is scientifically important because it corresponds to the air-quality exceedance level emphasized in the original ozone application.

The extended candidate set adds two families.  The first replaces the AR(1) time-varying coefficient structure with the AR(2) structure developed in this thesis.  The second adds MRTS covariates to selected Morris-type settings, so that nonstationary spatial structure can enter through multiple-resolution thin-plate spline basis functions.  These extensions are compared against the original Morris-type specifications using the same cross-validation splits, so the ranking reflects predictive performance rather than differences in train-test construction.

For each fitted model, posterior predictive samples at the validation sites are used to compute Brier scores and quantile scores.  The Brier scores are evaluated at upper-tail empirical thresholds $q(0.90)$, $q(0.95)$, $q(0.98)$, $q(0.99)$, and $q(0.995)$, and quantile scores are evaluated at the same probability levels.  Scores are averaged over validation sites, days, and folds.  Lower scores indicate better prediction.

Each MCMC chain is run for $30000$ iterations with $25000$ burn-in iterations.  As in the simulation study, convergence of prediction summaries is more important here than direct interpretation of partition-specific latent variables, because multiple-partition models can suffer from label switching.  The model-selection criterion is therefore based on held-out prediction scores, not on a visual comparison of latent partition labels.

\end{table}

To gauge how stable these rankings are to the train/validation split, we repeat the comparison at two smaller training sizes.  In the primary 400/400 split the empirical Brier thresholds at $0.90$, $0.95$, $0.98$, $0.99$, and $0.995$ are $73.25$, $79.75$, $88.50$, $94.75$, and $100.52$~ppb, and the top settings at these levels are numbered $204$, $111$, $58$, $68$, and $124$ in the candidate list.  When the split is changed to 300 training and 100 validation sites, the corresponding leaders become $204$, $70$, $204$, $117$, and $15$; with 200 training and 200 validation sites they become $54$, $117$, $215$, $58$, and $29$.  The same broad model families (Morris \skewt, AR(2), and MRTS variants) remain competitive across all three splits, but the exact best-ranked setting shifts with the split and the exceedance level.  Such instability is expected: at the $0.99$ and $0.995$ levels only a handful of validation observations exceed the threshold, so the ranking is sensitive to which sites happen to fall in the held-out set.  The real-data analysis therefore should not be read as identifying a single universally optimal specification, but as evidence that AR(2) and MRTS variants are valuable additions to the candidate pool when the inferential target is held-out tail prediction.

Overall, the real-data analysis provides a stronger practical motivation for the proposed extensions than the simulation study alone.  AR(2) yields additional temporal flexibility and appears among the leading models for several upper-tail quantile scores; MRTS enriches the spatial regression component and improves selected exceedance scores.  The final modelling recommendation, accordingly, is not to replace the Morris baseline by a single proposed specification, but to include AR(2) and MRTS variants in the candidate pool and to select among them using held-out Brier and quantile scores on the application of interest.

\section{MERRA-2 data analysis}

To complement the ozone case study with a controlled medium-size benchmark, we conduct a preliminary analysis on hourly surface-temperature output from the MERRA-2 reanalysis.  The objective is to assess whether the AR(2) temporal extension yields measurable predictive gains in a regime where the data are smoother and less tail-dominated than ground-level ozone.  The data comprise $n_s = 100$ spatial locations observed at $n_t = 100$ consecutive hourly time points, with longitude and latitude retained for each site.

\subsection{Data preprocessing and model setup}

We randomly split the sites into training and validation subsets using seed $123$, allocating $70\%$ of the sites to model fitting and the remaining $30\%$ to held-out evaluation.  The regression design uses an intercept and a first-order spatial trend,
\begin{align}
  \bX_t(\bs) = [1,\; s_1,\; s_2]^\top,
\end{align}
where $s_1$ and $s_2$ are the longitude and latitude of site $\bs$.  Because the covariates are not standardized in this experiment, we set \texttt{nu.upper = 10} so that the smoothness parameter $\nu$ of the \Matern covariance cannot drift into the numerically unstable large-$\nu$ regime during MCMC.

To isolate the effect of temporal structure, the three temporal specifications -- no time series, AR(1), and AR(2) -- share the same core configuration: a \skewt margin, a \Matern covariance, $K=5$ knots, and no censoring threshold.  Each chain is run for $20\,000$ iterations with the first $10\,000$ discarded as burn-in, yielding $10\,000$ retained posterior draws per model.

\subsection{Results}

Table~\ref{tab:merra2-temporal-compare} reports quantile-score (QS) and Brier-score (BS) comparisons across the three temporal specifications.  The differences are small in absolute terms but qualitatively informative: the no-time-series model performs marginally better on the QS metrics and attains the lowest BS at the most extreme exceedance level ($0.99$), whereas AR(2) attains the best BS at the $0.90$ and $0.95$ levels.  None of the three specifications therefore dominates uniformly; the preferred model depends on whether one targets bulk predictive accuracy, mid-tail exceedance probabilities, or the far tail.

The remaining summary metrics reinforce this reading.  MAPE is slightly lower for AR(2) ($0.0106$), with AR(1) close behind, while the no-time-series model is somewhat worse on this bulk-accuracy measure.  The $95\%$ predictive-interval coverage is nearly identical across the three models ($0.912$--$0.916$), suggesting that temporal dependence affects predictive sharpness more than calibration.  These modest gains come at a non-trivial computational cost: total fitting time rises from $777.3$ seconds for the no-time-series model to $1041.7$ seconds for AR(1) and $1075.8$ seconds for AR(2).  Taken together, the MERRA-2 results suggest that AR(2) is a defensible option when slightly better mid-tail behavior is desired, but the improvement is incremental rather than decisive -- consistent with the picture obtained from the simulation study.
