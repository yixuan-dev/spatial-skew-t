\section*{\centering 摘要}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{摘要} % 加入目錄

\hspace*{1.5em}空氣品質法規行之有年，汙染物濃度監測的目的，即在於判定各地是否超過法規標準。然而測站僅覆蓋少數地點，未設測站的地方則需要一套方法，量化其超過該標準的機率。本文將 Morris et al. (2017) 的時空 \skewt 過程沿兩軸擴展，一是潛在過程的 AR(2) 時間序列，二是作為迴歸共變數的多尺度薄板樣條 (MRTS) 基底。模型比較以 Brier 分數為依據。在模擬中，我們發現 AR(2) 擴展在第二階落後依賴較強時可改善未來日期的預報；MRTS 擴展則在非定態出現於均值曲面、且基底細緻到足以解析它時改善預測。於 2005 年 7 月的美國臭氧資料上，各模型的作用是以 AQS 測站觀測校正 CMAQ 數值模式輸出，兩項擴展在五個超標水準與三組訓練與驗證的資料切分下，皆維持在領先模型之列。MRTS 配適在所測試的每個水準上都優於基準，惟無任何單一增益能通過多重比較的校正。因此本文的貢獻在於界定這兩項擴展各自在何種預測任務與何種非定態下值得採用，並以超標預測表現作為取捨的依據。

\section*{\centering Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Abstract} % 加入目錄

Air quality regulation has long been enforced by monitoring whether
concentrations at a site exceed the standard. Monitors cover few locations,
however, and unmonitored sites call for a method that quantifies the
probability of exceeding that standard. We extend the spatio-temporal \skewt
process of Morris et al. (2017) along two axes, an AR(2) time series for
the latent processes and multi-resolution thin-plate spline (MRTS) basis
functions as regression covariates. Models are compared by Brier score. In
simulation, we find the AR(2) extension improves forecasts of future days under
strong second-lag dependence. The MRTS extension improves prediction where the
non-stationarity lies in the mean surface and the basis is fine enough to
resolve it. On July 2005 U.S. ozone, where each model calibrates
CMAQ model output against the AQS monitors, both extensions hold their place
among the leading models across five exceedance levels and three
train-validation splits. The MRTS fits improve on the baseline at every level
tested, though no single gain survives a correction for multiplicity. The
contribution is therefore to delimit the prediction tasks and the forms of
non-stationarity under which each extension is worth adopting, with exceedance
predictive performance as the criterion.

\vspace{2.0\baselineskip}

\noindent \textbf{Keywords}: spatio-temporal \skewt process; threshold exceedance prediction; AR(2) time series; multi-resolution thin-plate splines; Brier score; ozone

\section{Introduction}
\pagenumbering{arabic}                  % 阿拉伯數字
\setcounter{page}{1}                    % 將頁碼重設為 1
\WatermarkOn                            % 浮水印

Demand for accurate pollution-concentration estimates over broad spatial
domains continues to grow, driven by epidemiological evidence tying air quality
to morbidity and mortality and by tightening regulation, yet the cost and
logistics of a monitoring network cap the coverage that direct measurement can
provide. This gap motivates statistical methods
that extend a network's spatial reach with quantified confidence
\parencite{epa2024downscaler}. Ground-level ozone is the canonical regulatory
case. The National Ambient Air Quality Standard (NAAQS) specifies the maximum
concentration of ozone (O$_3$) allowed in outdoor air.  A site complies only if
the three-year average of its annual fourth-highest daily maximum eight-hour
concentration stays at or below the current NAAQS of $0.070$ parts per million
($70$ ppb), last revised in 2015. The Clean Air Act requires the EPA to review
the standard at least every five years; Table~\ref{tab:ozone-standards} lists
its successive revisions.
The policy-relevant target is thus not the mean field but its upper tail,
namely the probability that an unmonitored location breaches a high threshold;
our goal is to predict that probability at unmonitored sites and to map it
across the domain.

A spatial model for ozone threshold exceedances warrants special consideration,
since standard spatial methods are likely to perform poorly. Two features of the
problem are decisive. First, likelihood-based spatial modeling typically assumes
a Gaussian process, which is appropriate when interest lies in mean behavior. The
Gaussian distribution is light-tailed and symmetric. Daily ozone maxima are
neither. Their residuals are heavy-tailed and are well described by a \skewt
distribution \parencite{morris_space-time_2017}. Second, our interest is in the
dependence structure at high levels, which a covariance designed for mean
behavior does not capture. A Gaussian process is asymptotically independent
outside the degenerate case of perfect dependence, so the probability that two
sites are jointly extreme decays to zero as the level increases. Nearby ozone
monitors, however, tend to record high values on the same days, and this
agreement weakens with distance \parencite{morris_space-time_2017}. These
considerations call for a heavy-tailed, asymmetric margin that retains asymptotic
dependence. The \skewt distribution provides both. It is a subasymptotic model,
capturing the joint tail decay at high but finite levels rather than the limiting
dependence of extreme events \parencite{huser_advances_2022}. This is the setting
the ozone data occupy.

Our approach also departs from threshold models based on extreme-value
distributions, a well-developed part of the field in which extreme events are
defined as exceedances over a high threshold. \textcite{Davison1990} model
univariate exceedances with the generalized Pareto distribution.
\textcite{Ledford1996} address the multivariate case through a censored
likelihood that distinguishes the ways in which a threshold may be exceeded.
\textcite{Wadsworth2012} and \textcite{Thibaud2013} carried these ideas to
spatial extremes, fitting models by a censored pairwise likelihood
\parencite{Padoan2010}. \textcite{Huser2014} extended them to space-time data.
Such methods are grounded in extreme-value theory and presume a threshold high
enough for extremal models to be valid and for extrapolation beyond the observed
range. They are also computationally demanding and, in practice, restricted to
small datasets. Neither feature suits the present application, since the 75 ppb threshold
corresponds to roughly the 0.92 sample quantile of the daily ozone maxima
\parencite{morris_space-time_2017}, well short of the far tail that extremal
models require, and the network, at $1{,}089$ monitoring stations, is far larger
than such methods can handle. A complementary strategy retains the max-stable process while
reducing its computational cost. Fully Bayesian inference for max-stable
processes is cumbersome at scale. \textcite{morris_exploration_2019} address this
by truncating the spectral representation to a small set of empirical basis
functions that carry the spatial dependence. Their construction renders inference
feasible for large datasets and, as a by-product, dispenses with the assumption
of stationarity.

In this thesis we build on the spatial \skewt process of
\textcite{morris_space-time_2017}. This process is not max-stable, yet it retains
extremal dependence. Their model has four components. A \skewt margin, built by
location-scale mixing of Gaussian processes, supplies heavy tails and asymmetry.
A random spatial partition, redrawn each day,
confines asymptotic dependence to nearby sites. A censored likelihood focuses
inference on exceedances above a threshold. A transformed first-order
autoregressive process carries dependence through time. We extend this baseline
along two axes. On the temporal axis, the autoregressive prior links each day
only to the one before, and we add a further lag to capture longer-range
dependence in the record. On the spatial axis, the baseline assumes a stationary
\Matern covariance, restrictive over a continental network.
We therefore introduce the multi-resolution thin-plate spline basis of
\textcite{tzeng_resolution_2018} as fixed-effect covariates in the mean,
following the basis-function idea reviewed above, so as to relax stationarity at
little additional cost. Each axis is a candidate extension rather than a
replacement of the baseline, and together they are intended to improve prediction
of threshold exceedances.

The remainder of the thesis is organized as follows. \sref{sec:methodology}
reviews the spatial \skewt process of \textcite{morris_space-time_2017}, develops
the two extensions in turn (the standardized AR(2) prior on the latent dynamics
and the MRTS basis functions in the mean), and assembles them into a single
hierarchical model together with the MCMC algorithm used for inference.
\sref{sec:simulation} evaluates each extension on synthetic data, describing the
shared simulation design and the validation protocols, with the uncertainty
attached to each score comparison, and reporting the AR(2) and MRTS experiments
in turn.
\sref{sec:ozonedata} applies the models to the ozone monitoring data, comparing the
baseline against the two extensions in their ability to predict threshold
exceedances. \sref{sec:discussion} concludes and outlines directions for further
work. Technical derivations and design details are collected in the appendix.

\section{Methodology}\label{sec:methodology}

This section reviews the spatio-temporal skew-$t$ process (STP) of \textcite{morris_space-time_2017}, develops the AR(2) prior on the Gaussian-copula margins of its latent processes and the multi-resolution thin-plate spline (MRTS) augmentation of its regression mean, and assembles the full hierarchical model with the MCMC algorithm used for inference.

\subsection{The spatio-temporal skew-$t$ model}\label{sec:sttskewt}

Let $Y_t(\bs)$ denote the response at spatial location $\bs \in \calD \subset \calR^2$ on day $t \in \{1, \ldots, n_t\}$.  Following \textcite{morris_space-time_2017}, we model
\begin{align}
  Y_t(\bs) \;=\; \bX_t(\bs)^\top \bbeta \;+\; \lambda\, \sigma_t(\bs)\, |z_t(\bs)| \;+\; \sigma_t(\bs)\, v_t(\bs), \label{eq:fullmodel}
\end{align}
where $\bX_t(\bs)$ is a $p$-vector of covariates, $\bbeta$ is the corresponding regression coefficient vector, $\lambda \in \calR$ is a scalar skewness parameter, and $v_t(\bs)$ is a standard Gaussian process with stationary isotropic \Matern correlation
\begin{align}
  \rho(h) \;=\; (1 - \eta)\, I(h = 0) \;+\; \eta\, \frac{1}{\Gamma(\nu)\, 2^{\nu - 1}} \left( \sqrt{2\nu}\, \frac{h}{\varphi} \right)^{\nu} K_\nu\!\left( \sqrt{2\nu}\, \frac{h}{\varphi} \right), \label{eq:matern}
\end{align}
where $\varphi > 0$ is the spatial range, $\nu > 0$ is the smoothness, $\eta \in [0, 1]$ is the proportion of variance attributed to the spatial component, $K_\nu$ is the modified Bessel function of the second kind, and $h = \|\bs_1 - \bs_2\|$ is the Euclidean distance between sites $\bs_1$ and $\bs_2$.  Marginalizing over $|z_t(\bs)|$ and $\sigma_t(\bs)$, the finite-dimensional vector $\bY_t = [Y_t(\bs_1), \ldots, Y_t(\bs_n)]^\top$ follows an $n$-dimensional \skewt distribution; consequently \eref{eq:fullmodel} provides asymmetric and heavy-tailed margins together with asymptotic dependence between nearby sites.

\paragraph{Censoring.}
Because the EPA compliance criterion focuses on exceedances of a high level $L$, the model fits only the upper tail by censoring the data at a pre-specified threshold $T \le L$.  Writing the partially censored response $\widetilde{Y}_t(\bs) = \max\{Y_t(\bs), T\}$, inference is based on $\widetilde{\bY}_t = [\widetilde{Y}_t(\bs_1), \ldots, \widetilde{Y}_t(\bs_n)]^\top$, and the censored values below $T$ are imputed inside the MCMC.

\paragraph{Random Partition.}
To remove the long-range asymptotic dependence that arises when a single scalar $z$ and $\sigma^2$ are shared across the entire domain, the model uses a daily random partition.  For each day $t$, let $\bw_{t1}, \ldots, \bw_{tK}$ denote $K$ spatial knots in $\calD$, and define the partition subregions
\begin{align}
  P_{tk} \;=\; \{\bs \in \calD : k = \argmin_\ell \|\bs - \bw_{t\ell}\| \}, \qquad k = 1, \ldots, K.
\end{align}
For $\bs \in P_{tk}$ we set $z_t(\bs) = z_{tk}$ and $\sigma^2_t(\bs) = \sigma^2_{tk}$, with $z_{tk} \iid N(0, 1)$ and $\sigma^2_{tk} \iid \mathrm{IG}(a/2, b/2)$.  Within each subregion the model retains the \skewt form, while sites in different partitions are not asymptotically dependent.

\paragraph{Temporal Dependence.}
Daily temporal dependence is introduced on the latent parameters that drive the tail, namely the knot locations $\bw_{tk}$, the skewness latents $z_{tk}$, and the scale parameters $\sigma^2_{tk}$.  To preserve the marginal \skewt distribution at every day, each parameter is first transformed to a standard normal scale via the probability integral transform (PIT):
\begin{align}
  w^*_{tki} &= \Phi^{-1}\!\left[\frac{w_{tki} - \min(\bs_i)}{\max(\bs_i) - \min(\bs_i)}\right], \quad i = 1, 2, \label{eq:wstar}\\
  z^*_{tk}  &= \Phi^{-1}\{\mathrm{HN}(z_{tk})\}, \label{eq:zstar}\\
  \sigma^{2*}_{tk} &= \Phi^{-1}\{\mathrm{IG}(\sigma^2_{tk})\}, \label{eq:sigmastar}
\end{align}
where $\Phi$ is the standard normal distribution function, $\mathrm{HN}$ is the half-normal distribution function, and $\mathrm{IG}$ is the inverse-gamma distribution function with parameters $(a/2, b/2)$.  After transformation, each $\bw^*_{tk}, z^*_{tk}, \sigma^{2*}_{tk}$ has a standard normal marginal distribution, and \textcite{morris_space-time_2017} place a stationary AR(1) time series on each of them:
\begin{align}
  \bw^*_{tk} \mid \bw^*_{t-1, k} &\sim N_2\!\left(\phi^{(w)}\, \bw^*_{t-1, k},\; (1 - (\phi^{(w)})^2)\, \bI_2\right), \label{eq:ar1-w}\\
  z^*_{tk} \mid z^*_{t-1, k} &\sim N\!\left(\phi^{(z)}\, z^*_{t-1, k},\; 1 - (\phi^{(z)})^2\right), \label{eq:ar1-z}\\
  \sigma^{2*}_{tk} \mid \sigma^{2*}_{t-1, k} &\sim N\!\left(\phi^{(\sigma)}\, \sigma^{2*}_{t-1, k},\; 1 - (\phi^{(\sigma)})^2\right), \label{eq:ar1-sigma}
\end{align}
with $|\phi^{(w)}|, |\phi^{(z)}|, |\phi^{(\sigma)}| < 1$, and initial values $\bw^*_{1k} \sim N_2(\bZero, \bI_2)$, $z^*_{1k} \sim N(0, 1)$, $\sigma^{2*}_{1k} \sim N(0, 1)$.  Transforming back through \eref{eq:wstar}--\eref{eq:sigmastar} gives $\bw_{tk} \sim \mathrm{Unif}(\calD)$, $z_{tk} \sim \mathrm{HN}(0, 1)$, and $\sigma^2_{tk} \sim \mathrm{IG}(a/2, b/2)$ at every $t$, so that the spatial \skewt structure of \eref{eq:fullmodel} is preserved at each day.

\subsection{Extremal dependence}\label{sec:extdep}

For exceedance prediction, what matters is how strongly the \skewt process couples extreme values at two sites, and how that coupling changes with their separation.
One measure of this extremal dependence is the $\chi$ statistic \parencite{Coles1999}, which for a stationary and isotropic spatial process at separation $h$ is
\begin{align} \label{eq:chih}
  \chi(h) = \lim_{c \rightarrow c^*} \Pr[Y(\bs + h) > c \mid Y(\bs) > c],
\end{align}
where $c^*$ is the upper limit of the support of $Y$; for the \skewt distribution $c^* = \infty$.
If $\chi(h) = 0$, then observations are asymptotically independent at distance $h$.
For Gaussian processes, $\chi(h) = 0$ regardless of the distance $h$, so they are not suitable for modeling asymptotically dependent extremes.
Unlike the Gaussian process, the \skewt process is asymptotically dependent.
However, one problem with the spatial \skewt process is that $\lim_{h \rightarrow \infty} \chi(h) > 0$.
This occurs because all observations, both near and far, share the same $z$ and $\sigma$ terms.
Such long-range dependence is undesirable over large domains, where only local dependence is expected.
\textcite{morris_space-time_2017} avoid this problem through a random partitioning mechanism, described in detail in \sref{sec:sttskewt}.

\subsection{Extension to AR(2) latent processes}\label{sec:ar2}

In this section, we generalize the AR(1) prior \eref{eq:ar1-w}--\eref{eq:ar1-sigma} on the transformed latents to a stationary AR(2) prior.

\paragraph{The AR(2) prior.}
Writing $\{Y_t\}$ for any scalar component of $\bw^*_{tk}$, or for $z^*_{tk}$ or $\sigma^{2*}_{tk}$ at a fixed knot $k$, the prior is the stationary AR(2) recursion
\begin{align}
  Y_t \;=\; \phi_1\, Y_{t-1} \;+\; \phi_2\, Y_{t-2} \;+\; \epsilon_t,
  \qquad \epsilon_t \iid N(0, \sigma_\epsilon^2),
  \qquad t \ge 3, \label{eq:ar2-recursion}
\end{align}
with $(\phi_1, \phi_2)$ confined to the stationarity triangle
\begin{align}
  \calS \;=\; \big\{ (\phi_1, \phi_2) \;:\; |\phi_2| < 1, \;\; \phi_1 + \phi_2 < 1, \;\; \phi_2 - \phi_1 < 1 \big\}. \label{eq:stab-triangle}
\end{align}
Because the inverse PIT \eref{eq:wstar}--\eref{eq:sigmastar} returns the half-normal, inverse-gamma, and uniform target margins only when $\gamma_0 := \mathrm{Var}(Y_t) = 1$, the innovation variance is not free.  Solving the Yule--Walker equations under $\gamma_0 = 1$ (Appendix~\ref{app:ar2-moments}) fixes the standardized autocovariances
\begin{align}
  \gamma_1 \;=\; \frac{\phi_1}{1 - \phi_2}, \qquad
  \gamma_2 \;=\; \phi_1\, \gamma_1 + \phi_2, \label{eq:gamma12}
\end{align}
\begin{align}
  \sigma_\epsilon^2 \;=\; 1 - \phi_1\, \gamma_1 - \phi_2\, \gamma_2. \label{eq:sigeps}
\end{align}
Setting $\phi_2 = 0$ gives $\gamma_1 = \phi_1$ and $\sigma_\epsilon^2 = 1 - \phi_1^2$, the AR(1) form of \eref{eq:ar1-w}--\eref{eq:ar1-sigma}, so the AR(2) prior strictly nests the prior of \textcite{morris_space-time_2017}.

\paragraph{Stationary initial pair.}
The recursion \eref{eq:ar2-recursion} needs two seeds, and independent $N(0, 1)$ seeds would leave $\mathrm{Var}(Y_t) \ne 1$ over the first days and break the margin.  We therefore draw the initial pair from the stationary bivariate Gaussian
\begin{align}
  \begin{pmatrix} Y_1 \\ Y_2 \end{pmatrix}
  \;\sim\;
  N_2\!\left(
    \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix},\;
    \begin{pmatrix} 1 & \gamma_1 \\ \gamma_1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}
  \right), \label{eq:joint-init}
\end{align}
so that $\mathrm{Var}(Y_t) = 1$ holds from $t = 1$ onward.

\paragraph{Application to the three transformed parameters.}
Mirroring the AR(1) recursions \eref{eq:ar1-w}--\eref{eq:ar1-sigma}, the AR(2) prior \eref{eq:ar2-recursion} with initial condition \eref{eq:joint-init} replaces it component-wise:
\begin{align}
  \bw^*_{tk} \mid \bw^*_{t-1, k}, \bw^*_{t-2, k}
    &\sim N_2\!\left(\phi^{(w)}_1\, \bw^*_{t-1, k} + \phi^{(w)}_2\, \bw^*_{t-2, k},\; \sigma_{\epsilon, w}^2\, \bI_2\right), \label{eq:ar2-w}\\
  z^*_{tk} \mid z^*_{t-1, k}, z^*_{t-2, k}
    &\sim N\!\left(\phi^{(z)}_1\, z^*_{t-1, k} + \phi^{(z)}_2\, z^*_{t-2, k},\; \sigma_{\epsilon, z}^2\right), \label{eq:ar2-z}\\
  \sigma^{2*}_{tk} \mid \sigma^{2*}_{t-1, k}, \sigma^{2*}_{t-2, k}
    &\sim N\!\left(\phi^{(\sigma)}_1\, \sigma^{2*}_{t-1, k} + \phi^{(\sigma)}_2\, \sigma^{2*}_{t-2, k},\; \sigma_{\epsilon, \sigma}^2\right), \label{eq:ar2-sigma}
\end{align}
where each pair $\bphi^{(\cdot)}$ lies in the stationarity triangle $\calS$ of \eref{eq:stab-triangle}, the innovation variances follow from \eref{eq:gamma12}--\eref{eq:sigeps}, and the initial pairs follow \eref{eq:joint-init} component-wise.  After the inverse PIT \eref{eq:wstar}--\eref{eq:sigmastar}, the back-transformed $\bw_{tk}, z_{tk}, \sigma^2_{tk}$ keep the same marginals as the AR(1) baseline, so the spatial \skewt model \eref{eq:fullmodel} is preserved at every $t$.  The Metropolis step for each $Y_t$ now targets a full conditional carrying three prior factors (self, lag-1, lag-2); its explicit form and the Metropolis--Hastings acceptance ratio are given in Appendix~\ref{app:full-cond}.

\subsection{Incorporating MRTS covariates}\label{sec:meth-mrts}

\textcite{tzeng_resolution_2018} proposed the multi-resolution thin-plate spline (MRTS) basis, whose functions are ordered from coarse to increasingly fine resolution. This ordering lets a model add spatial detail one resolution at a time; we define the basis below on a $2$-dimensional spatial domain. Consider $n$ distinct locations, $\bs_1, \cdots, \bs_n$, where $\bs_i = (x_{i1}, x_{i2})^\top \in \calR^2$ for $i = 1, \ldots, n$, and let
\begin{equation}
  \bR \;=\;
  \begin{pmatrix}
    1 & x_{11} & x_{12} \\
    \vdots & \vdots & \vdots \\
    1 & x_{n1} & x_{n2}
  \end{pmatrix}.
  \label{eq:mrts-X}
\end{equation}
Then the MRTS basis functions $f_k(\bs)$ at a location $\bs = (x_1, x_2)^\top$ are defined as
\begin{equation}
  f_k(\bs) \;=\;
  \begin{cases}
    1, & k = 1, \\
    x_{k-1}, & k = 2, 3, \\
    \mu_{k-3}^{-1} \big\{ \bpsi(\bs) \,-\, \bPsi \bR( \bR^\top \bR )^{-1} \, \br \big\}^\top \bv_{k-3}, & k = 4, \ldots, n,
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:mrts-basis}
\end{equation}
where $\br = (1, x_1, x_2)^\top$, $\bpsi(\bs) = (\psi_1(\bs), \ldots, \psi_n(\bs))^\top$ with
\begin{equation}
  \psi_i(\bs) \;=\; \frac{1}{8\pi}\,\|\bs - \bs_i\|^2 \, \log\!\big(\|\bs - \bs_i\|\big), \qquad i = 1, \ldots, n,
  \label{eq:tps-kernel}
\end{equation}
and $\bPsi$ is the $n \times n$ matrix with $(i,j)$-th element $\psi_j(\bs_i)$, $\bv_k$ is the $k$-th column of $\bV$, and $\bV\,\mathrm{diag}(\mu_1, \ldots, \mu_n)\,\bV^\top$ is the eigen-decomposition of $\bQ\,\bPsi\,\bQ$ with $\mu_1 \ge \cdots \ge \mu_n$ and $\bQ = \bI - \bR(\bR^\top \bR)^{-1} \bR^\top$.

The MRTS basis enters the STP through the regression component. Fix the number of basis functions $K_{\mathrm{MRTS}} \ge 1$ and let
\begin{align}
 \bff(\bs) \;=\; \big(f_1(\bs),\, f_2(\bs),\, \ldots,\, f_{K_{\mathrm{MRTS}}}(\bs)\big)^\top
 \label{eq:mrts-f}
\end{align}
collect the first $K_{\mathrm{MRTS}}$ basis functions defined in \eref{eq:mrts-basis}. Then the day-$t$ covariate vector $\bX_t(\bs)$ in \eref{eq:fullmodel} is replaced by
\begin{align}
 \bX^*_t(\bs)
 &= \big[\bff(\bs)^\top,\; \bX_t(\bs)^\top\big]^\top \notag\\
 &= \big[f_1(\bs),\; f_2(\bs),\, \ldots,\, f_{K_{\mathrm{MRTS}}}(\bs),\; \mathrm{X}_1(\bs, t),\, \ldots,\, \mathrm{X}_p(\bs, t)\big]^\top,
 \label{eq:mrts-augmented}
\end{align}
where $\mathrm{X}_j(\bs, t)$ represents the $j$-th exogenous covariate of $\bX_t(\bs)$. The model in \eref{eq:fullmodel} can then be expressed as
\begin{align}
  Y_t(\bs) \;=\; \bX^*_t(\bs)^\top \bbeta^* \;+\; \lambda\, \sigma_t(\bs)\, |z_t(\bs)| \;+\; \sigma_t(\bs)\, v_t(\bs),
  \label{eq:fullmodel-mrts}
\end{align}
which is identical to the STP model \eref{eq:fullmodel} on all components other than the mean, with $\bbeta^* \in \calR^{K_{\mathrm{MRTS}} + p}$.  Because the MRTS basis functions depend only on location, each site $\bs$ shares the same ordered set of basis values $\bff(\bs)$ at every day $t$.  The number of basis functions $K_{\mathrm{MRTS}}$ is the practical tuning parameter.  \sref{sec:sim-mrts-design} compares $K_{\mathrm{MRTS}} \in \{0, 3, 5, 8, 10, 12, 15, 20, 25\}$ across its simulation designs, extended by $\{30, 40, 50\}$ on the two nonlinear-mean designs.

\subsection{Hierarchical model}\label{sec:meth-hier}

We specify the model as a three-stage Bayesian hierarchical model in the sense
of \textcite{berliner_hierarchical_1996}, following the presentation of hierarchical spatial models
in \textcite{gelfand_handbook_2010}, namely a data stage conditional on the
latent process, a process stage conditional on the parameters, and a parameter
stage carrying the priors.  Collect the day-$t$ latent processes as
$\calZ_t = \{(\bw_{tk},\, z_{tk},\, \sigma^2_{tk}) : k = 1, \ldots, K\}$ and the
parameters as $\btheta = \{\bbeta, \lambda, \varphi, \nu, \eta, a, b, \bphi\}$,
where $\bphi$ collects the AR coefficients of the three latent processes.  The
number of knots $K$ is not treated as random; it indexes candidate models
selected by held-out Brier score (\sref{sec:sim-scoring}).

\paragraph{Stage 1: data model.}
The observations are the censored responses,
\begin{align}
  \widetilde{Y}_t(\bs_i) \;=\; \max\{Y_t(\bs_i),\, T\},
  \qquad i = 1, \ldots, n,\quad t = 1, \ldots, n_t,
  \label{eq:stage1-censor}
\end{align}
so that values above $T$ are observed exactly, while values below $T$ are known
only to lie below $T$ and are imputed within the MCMC.

\paragraph{Stage 2: process model.}
The first substage is the conditional Gaussian field: independently across days,
\begin{align}
  \bY_t \mid \calZ_t,\, \btheta
  \;\sim\;
  N_n\!\Big(\bX_t\bbeta \,+\, \lambda\, \bD_t\, |\bz_t|,\;\;
            \bD_t\, \bR(\varphi, \nu, \eta)\, \bD_t\Big),
  \label{eq:bhm-data-gauss}
\end{align}
where $\bD_t = \mathrm{diag}\{\sigma_t(\bs_1), \ldots, \sigma_t(\bs_n)\}$,
$|\bz_t| = (|z_t(\bs_1)|, \ldots, |z_t(\bs_n)|)^\top$, and
$\bR(\varphi, \nu, \eta)$ has entries $\rho(\|\bs_i - \bs_j\|)$ from
\eref{eq:matern}.  Under the MRTS extension, $\bX_t\bbeta$ is replaced by
$\bX^*_t\bbeta^*$ of \eref{eq:mrts-augmented}.

The second substage maps the subregion latents to the sites through the
partition: for $\bs \in P_{tk}$,
\begin{align}
  z_t(\bs) \;=\; z_{tk},
  \qquad
  \sigma^2_t(\bs) \;=\; \sigma^2_{tk},
  \qquad
  P_{tk} \;=\; \{\bs \in \calD : k = \argmin_\ell \|\bs - \bw_{t\ell}\|\}.
  \label{eq:stage2-partition}
\end{align}

The third substage gives the latent temporal dynamics on the transformed scale
of \eref{eq:wstar}--\eref{eq:sigmastar}, independently across knots
$k = 1, \ldots, K$: for $t \ge 3$,
\begin{align}
  \bw^*_{tk} \mid \bw^*_{t-1,k},\, \bw^*_{t-2,k},\, \bphi^{(w)}
    &\sim N_2\!\big(\phi^{(w)}_1 \bw^*_{t-1,k} + \phi^{(w)}_2 \bw^*_{t-2,k},\;
                    \sigma^2_{\epsilon,w}\, \bI_2\big),
    \label{eq:stage2-w}\\
  z^*_{tk} \mid z^*_{t-1,k},\, z^*_{t-2,k},\, \bphi^{(z)}
    &\sim N\!\big(\phi^{(z)}_1 z^*_{t-1,k} + \phi^{(z)}_2 z^*_{t-2,k},\;
                  \sigma^2_{\epsilon,z}\big),
    \label{eq:stage2-z}\\
  \sigma^{2*}_{tk} \mid \sigma^{2*}_{t-1,k},\, \sigma^{2*}_{t-2,k},\, \bphi^{(\sigma)}
    &\sim N\!\big(\phi^{(\sigma)}_1 \sigma^{2*}_{t-1,k}
                  + \phi^{(\sigma)}_2 \sigma^{2*}_{t-2,k},\;
                  \sigma^2_{\epsilon,\sigma}\big),
    \label{eq:stage2-sigma}
\end{align}
with each innovation variance fixed by the Yule--Walker standardization
\eref{eq:gamma12}--\eref{eq:sigeps} and each initial pair drawn from the
stationary bivariate Gaussian \eref{eq:joint-init}.  The AR(1) baseline of
\textcite{morris_space-time_2017} is the special case $\phi^{(\cdot)}_2 = 0$,
which reduces \eref{eq:stage2-w}--\eref{eq:stage2-sigma} to
\eref{eq:ar1-w}--\eref{eq:ar1-sigma}.  The inverse transforms
\eref{eq:wstar}--\eref{eq:sigmastar} return the marginals
$\bw_{tk} \sim \mathrm{Unif}(\calD)$, $z_{tk} \sim \mathrm{HN}(0,1)$, and
$\sigma^2_{tk} \sim \mathrm{IG}(a/2,\, b/2)$ at every $t$.

\paragraph{Stage 3: parameter model.}
All parameters, and only parameters, receive priors at this stage:
\begin{align}
  \bbeta &\sim N_p(\bZero,\, \sigma^2_\beta \bI),
  &
  \lambda &\sim N(0,\, \sigma^2_\lambda),
  &
  \eta &\sim \mathrm{Unif}(0, 1),
  \nonumber\\
  \varphi &\sim \mathrm{Unif}(0,\, \varphi_{\max}),
  &
  a/2 &\sim \mathrm{Unif}\{0.05,\, 0.10,\, \ldots,\, 5\},
  &
  b/2 &\sim \mathrm{Gamma}(1,\, 1),
  \nonumber\\
  \log \nu &\sim N(-1.2,\, 1),\ \nu \le 10,
  &
  \bphi^{(\cdot)}
    &\sim N_2\big(\bZero,\, \sigma^2_\phi \bI_2\big)\,
      I\big(\bphi^{(\cdot)} \in \calS\big).
  \label{eq:stage3-priors}
\end{align}
Here $\mathrm{Unif}\{\cdot\}$ denotes the discrete uniform distribution on the
listed grid, and the hyperparameters are $\sigma_\beta = \sigma_\lambda = 20$
and $\sigma_\phi = 0.5$,
with the range bound $\varphi_{\max} = 15$ in the simulation study and
$\varphi_{\max} = 5$ in the ozone study, matched to the extent of each spatial
domain.  The smoothness $\nu$ receives a lognormal prior with median
$e^{-1.2} \approx 0.30$, truncated above at $10$.  Both autoregressive
coefficients carry the
prior scale $\sigma_\phi$ of the AR(1) baseline, so the AR(1) and AR(2) fits
are matched on prior scale rather than tuned separately.  One set of fits
departs from \eref{eq:stage3-priors}, namely the time-block forecast fits of
\sref{sec:sim-ar2-design}, which truncate the prior on $\lambda$ to the
positive half line for an identifiability reason given there; every other fit
reported in this thesis uses \eref{eq:stage3-priors} as stated.

Writing $[\,\cdot\,]$ for a generic distribution, the three stages multiply to
the joint model, and by Bayes' Rule the posterior is proportional to that
product,
\begin{align}
  \big[\bY_{\mathrm{cens}},\, \calZ,\, \btheta \mid \widetilde{\bY}\big]
  \;\propto\;
  \prod_{t=1}^{n_t}
  \big[\widetilde{\bY}_t \mid \bY_t\big]\,
  \big[\bY_t \mid \calZ_t,\, \btheta\big]\;
  \big[\calZ_{1:n_t} \mid \btheta\big]\,
  \big[\btheta\big],
  \label{eq:bhm-posterior}
\end{align}
sampled by a Gibbs scan that draws directly from every closed-form full
conditional, namely those of $\bbeta$, $\lambda$, $a/2$, $b/2$, and the
censored responses.  The remaining blocks are updated by Metropolis--Hastings
steps, namely the latent processes on the transformed scale of
\eref{eq:wstar}--\eref{eq:sigmastar}, the autoregressive coefficients, and the
\Matern parameters.  A Gibbs scan of this kind, with Metropolis--Hastings steps
standing in for the intractable draws, is a Metropolis-within-Gibbs sampler.
Each factor of
\eref{eq:bhm-posterior} is one of the stages above, and the full conditionals
are read off stage by stage; the full conditionals of the time-varying
parameters and of the autoregressive coefficients, together with their
Metropolis--Hastings acceptance ratios, are given in
Appendix~\ref{app:full-cond}.  The predictive distributions that this
posterior induces are given in \sref{sec:meth-pred}.

\subsection{Posterior predictive distribution}\label{sec:meth-pred}

The two validation protocols of \sref{sec:sim-scoring} both score a posterior
predictive distribution, and they differ in which of its factors the data can
inform.  Write $\widetilde{\bY}_{1:n_o}$ for the record observed up to time
$n_o$, let $\bY^{\mathrm{new}}$ collect the responses to be predicted, and let
$\calZ^{\mathrm{new}}$ collect whatever latent processes those responses
require beyond $\calZ_{1:n_o}$.  Integrating the joint model
\eref{eq:bhm-posterior} over the posterior gives
\begin{align}
  \big[\bY^{\mathrm{new}} \mid \widetilde{\bY}_{1:n_o}\big]
  \;=\;
  \int
  \underbrace{\big[\bY^{\mathrm{new}} \mid \calZ^{\mathrm{new}},\, \calZ_{1:n_o},\, \btheta\big]}_{\text{data model}}\,
  \underbrace{\big[\calZ^{\mathrm{new}} \mid \calZ_{1:n_o},\, \btheta\big]}_{\text{latent transition}}\,
  \underbrace{\big[\calZ_{1:n_o},\, \btheta \mid \widetilde{\bY}_{1:n_o}\big]}_{\text{posterior}}
  \;\dd\calZ^{\mathrm{new}}\, \dd\calZ_{1:n_o}\, \dd\btheta .
  \label{eq:postpred}
\end{align}
Only the third factor is a posterior.  The second is the AR(2) prior
\eref{eq:stage2-w}--\eref{eq:stage2-sigma} of the process stage, which the data
reach only through $\bphi$ and through the states it is conditioned on.  Which
of the two remaining factors carries the prediction is what separates the
protocols.

\paragraph{Prediction at an unmonitored site.}
When the target is a day $t \le n_o$ at a site $\bs_0$ that was not
monitored, $\calZ^{\mathrm{new}}$ is empty and the transition factor drops out
of \eref{eq:postpred}.  Day $t$ is observed at the monitored sites, so
$z_{tk}$ and $\sigma^2_{tk}$ are already part of $\calZ_{1:n_o}$ and carry a
posterior of their own.  Within a draw, $Y_t(\bs_0)$ then follows the Gaussian
conditional of \eref{eq:bhm-data-gauss} given the monitored responses of that
day, whose mean adds to $\bX_t(\bs_0)^\top\bbeta + \lambda \sigma_t(\bs_0)
|z_t(\bs_0)|$ a term proportional to the day-$t$ residuals at the monitored
sites.  This is Bayesian kriging, and the cross-sectional information of the
day being predicted enters through that term.

\paragraph{Forecast beyond the end of the record.}
When the target is a day $n_o + h$, no site is observed on that day, the
kriging term of the previous paragraph has nothing to condition on, and
$\calZ^{\mathrm{new}} = \calZ_{n_o+1:n_o+H}$ must be supplied entirely by the
transition factor.  The AR(2) recursion is therefore run forward from the two
seam states $\calZ_{n_o-1}$ and $\calZ_{n_o}$ of the same draw, on the
transformed scale of \eref{eq:wstar}--\eref{eq:sigmastar} where the recursion
is defined, and the inverse transforms return the latents to the scale on
which \eref{eq:bhm-data-gauss} acts.  Because the autocorrelations
\eref{eq:gamma12} decay in $h$, the forecast distribution relaxes toward the
stationary margins of \eref{eq:wstar}--\eref{eq:sigmastar} as the lead grows,
and the persistence of the fitted process governs how quickly.  A model whose
latent processes are independent in time is the limiting case in which each
lead is drawn from those margins directly, so under that model the forecast
carries no information from the record beyond $\btheta$.

Algorithm~\ref{alg:forecast} states the Monte Carlo form of the forecast.  Each
retained draw contributes one path, so parameter uncertainty and seam-state
uncertainty are both propagated and no posterior summary is substituted for
either.  Conditioning on the seam states of the draw, rather than on the
stationary distribution of the latent processes, is what makes the AR(1) and
AR(2) forecasts differ from the independent one at short leads.  The
exceedance probability $\widehat{P}(Y > L)$ required by the Brier score of
\sref{sec:sim-scoring} is the proportion of the returned paths that exceed $L$
at the site and lead being scored.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption[Posterior predictive forecast over a held-out window]{Posterior
predictive forecast over the forecast window $(n_o,\, n_o + H]$.}
\label{alg:forecast}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
  \For{each retained posterior draw $m = 1, \ldots, M$}
    \State take $\btheta^{(m)}$ and the seam states
           $\calZ^{(m)}_{n_o-1}, \calZ^{(m)}_{n_o}$ from the MCMC output
    \State transform the seam states to the Gaussian scale by the PIT
           \eref{eq:wstar}--\eref{eq:sigmastar}
    \For{$h = 1, \ldots, H$}
      \State draw $\bw^*, z^*, \sigma^{2*}$ at lead $h$ from the AR(2)
             recursion \eref{eq:stage2-w}--\eref{eq:stage2-sigma} at
             $\bphi^{(m)}$, with the innovation variances
             \eref{eq:sigeps}
      \State invert the PIT to obtain $\calZ^{(m)}_{n_o+h}$
      \State draw $\bY^{(m)}_{n_o+h}$ from the conditional Gaussian field
             \eref{eq:bhm-data-gauss} at $\calZ^{(m)}_{n_o+h}$ and
             $\btheta^{(m)}$
    \EndFor
  \EndFor
  \State \Return the predictive sample
         $\{\bY^{(m)}_{n_o+1:n_o+H}\}_{m=1}^{M}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Line 4 of Algorithm~\ref{alg:forecast} advances the knot locations along with
the scale and skewness latents, and line 6 places every site in the subregion
of \eref{eq:stage2-partition} that its forecast knots define.  At $K = 1$ the
partition is the whole domain at every lead, so the knot locations leave the
field unchanged and only $z$ and $\sigma^2$ carry the temporal signal into the
forecast.  The forecast evaluation of \sref{sec:sim-ar2-design} is set at
$K = 1$ for that reason, which also gives the AR(2), AR(1), and independent
fits a common partition to be compared on.

\section{Simulation study}\label{sec:simulation}

This simulation study examines whether, and under which data-generating settings, the AR(2) and MRTS extensions improve held-out exceedance prediction.  \sref{sec:sim-design} gives the design shared by all experiments and \sref{sec:sim-scoring} the validation protocols, including the uncertainty attached to the paired score comparisons.  \sref{sec:sim-ar2-design} evaluates the AR(2) extension under autoregressive and long-memory temporal dynamics; \sref{sec:sim-mrts-design} evaluates the MRTS extension under second-moment and first-moment spatial non-stationarity.  Throughout, both extensions are assessed as candidate options, benchmarked against Gaussian fits and against matched models without each extension.

\subsection{Common simulation design}\label{sec:sim-design}

All simulation designs generate data from the hierarchical model of \sref{sec:meth-hier} on $n_s = 144$ sites, drawn uniformly on $[0,10] \times [0,10]$, over $n_t = 50$ days ($n_t = 200$ in the time-block forecast evaluation of \sref{sec:sim-ar2-design}).  The two nonlinear-mean designs of \sref{sec:sim-mrts-design} are the single exception, replacing the \skewt error process with a Gaussian one.  In every design the \Matern correlation \eref{eq:matern} is set to $\eta = 0.9$, $\nu = 1/2$, and $\varphi = 1$.  In the data generation the regression mean is held constant at $\bX^\top \bbeta = 10$, while every fitted model includes an intercept and a first-order spatial trend.  The designs of \sref{sec:sim-mrts-design} depart from that constant mean in two ways.  One adds a mean-zero non-stationary random effect, which leaves the regression mean at $10$; the two Gaussian-error designs carry a fixed nonlinear mean surface in its place.  For each design we simulate $10$ data sets and run every MCMC chain for $20{,}000$ iterations, discarding the first $10{,}000$ as burn-in.  The spatial hold-out evaluation splits each data set into $100$ training sites and $44$ withheld test sites, while the time-block forecast evaluation observes all $144$ (\sref{sec:sim-scoring}).  Throughout, $q(0.80)$ denotes the $80$th sample quantile of the data, and a threshold $T = q(0.80)$ means censoring the response there.  The five baseline analysis models are
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=3em]
  \item Gaussian marginal, $K = 1$ knot;
  \item \Skewt marginal, $K = 1$ knot, no thresholding;
  \item Symmetric-$t$ marginal, $K = 1$ knot, threshold $T = q(0.80)$;
  \item \Skewt marginal, $K = 5$ knots, no thresholding;
  \item Symmetric-$t$ marginal, $K = 5$ knots, threshold $T = q(0.80)$.
\end{enumerate}

Censoring is paired with the symmetric-$t$ marginal because the skewness parameter is only weakly identified once the lower tail is censored, following \textcite{morris_space-time_2017}.

\subsection{Validation protocols and uncertainty}\label{sec:sim-scoring}

\paragraph{Spatial hold-out validation.}
Each setting is fit to the $100$ training sites of a replicate and scored at its $44$ withheld sites.  Because the target is exceedance of a high level, we compare models by the Brier score \parencite{gneiting_strictly_2007}.  For an exceedance level $L$, the Brier score is
\begin{align}
  \mathrm{BS}(L) \;=\; \big\{\, I(y > L) \;-\; \widehat{P}(Y > L) \,\big\}^2,
  \label{eq:brier-score}
\end{align}
where $y$ is the held-out observation, $I(y > L)$ is the exceedance indicator, and $\widehat{P}(Y > L)$ is the posterior predictive probability of exceeding $L$.  We average the Brier scores over all test sites and days; lower values indicate better exceedance prediction, and $L$ ranges over the sample quantiles reported with each figure.

\paragraph{Time-block forecast validation.}\label{sec:sim-timeblock-cv}

The site-splitting scheme above leaves the day being predicted observed at the training sites, so its predictive distribution keeps the kriging term of \sref{sec:meth-pred} and borrows from that day.  The time-block forecast evaluation of \sref{sec:sim-ar2-design} withdraws that support and scores the forecast of Algorithm~\ref{alg:forecast} instead.  All $144$ sites are observed up to a forecast origin, the next $15$ days are predicted at those same sites, and no observation after the origin is available anywhere.  Those $15$ days are one forecast window.  Each data set supplies five origins, at days $50$, $80$, $110$, $140$ and $170$ of a $200$-day record, so the evaluation does not rest on a single stretch of the record.  For each forecast lead $h$ the Brier score \eref{eq:brier-score} is computed with $L$ set to the empirical quantile of that data set's whole record, which is the rule the spatial hold-out already uses, so the two evaluations are measured on one scale.  Results are reported at the $0.90$ and $0.95$ quantiles and over two lead windows, leads $1$--$5$ and leads $1$--$15$.  Scores are averaged over sites, over the leads of the reported lead window, and over the five forecast windows, leaving one number per data set, and that number is the unit of the paired comparisons below.

\paragraph{Uncertainty of score comparisons.}\label{sec:sim-uq}

A mean Brier score is an estimate, and two such estimates computed on the same held-out sites are strongly positively correlated, since a site that is hard to predict is hard for every model.  We therefore attach uncertainty to the \emph{paired difference} of scores.  For a matched pair of models $A$ and $B$ and an exceedance level $L$, the estimand is
\begin{align}
  \Delta(L) \;=\; \mathrm{E}\big[\mathrm{BS}_A(L)\big] \;-\; \mathrm{E}\big[\mathrm{BS}_B(L)\big],
  \label{eq:delta-estimand}
\end{align}
estimated by forming the difference site by site on the shared held-out set and assessing its sampling variability over the $10$ simulated data sets.  Each comparison tests
\begin{align}
  H_0\!:\; \Delta(L) = 0 \qquad \text{against} \qquad H_1\!:\; \Delta(L) \neq 0,
  \label{eq:delta-null}
\end{align}
summarized by a $95\%$ paired-$t$ confidence interval and a Wilcoxon signed-rank test, which does not assume normal differences; with $10$ replicates the Wilcoxon $p$-values move on a coarse grid whose smallest attainable two-sided value is $2^{-9} \approx 0.002$.  Each pair varies only the factor under test, with the analysis knot count matched to the generating design.  In the tables that follow, a star marks an interval that excludes zero.  Among the dozens of paired comparisons reported in this thesis, an isolated borderline star is within what chance alone would produce.  An interval that covers zero means the data fail to reject $H_0$, not that the two models have been shown equal.

\subsection{The AR(2) experiment}\label{sec:sim-ar2-design}

\paragraph{Design.}
To test the AR(2) analysis model against data with a known autoregressive structure, the six generating designs are \skewt ($\lambda = 3$) with temporal dependence imposed on the latent $\sigma^2$, $\bw$, and $z$ processes.  The first five evolve through a fixed AR(2) recursion \eref{eq:ar2-w}--\eref{eq:ar2-sigma}; the sixth replaces it with a long-memory ARFIMA$(0, d, 0)$ process:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=3em]
  \item $K = 1$ knot, strong dependence $(\phi_1, \phi_2) = (0.80, -0.35)$;
  \item $K = 1$ knot, weak dependence $(\phi_1, \phi_2) = (0.12, -0.05)$;
  \item $K = 5$ knots, strong dependence $(\phi_1, \phi_2) = (0.80, -0.35)$;
  \item $K = 5$ knots, weak dependence $(\phi_1, \phi_2) = (0.12, -0.05)$;
  \item $K = 1$ knot, AR(2) $(\phi_1, \phi_2) = (0.15, 0.80)$;
  \item $K = 1$ knot, long-memory ARFIMA$(0, d, 0)$, $d = 0.45$ \parencite{davies_tests_1987}.
\end{enumerate}
All three AR(2) coefficient pairs lie inside the stationarity triangle $\calS$.  Every design is fit by the same nine analysis models, namely the five baselines and the \skewt models with AR(1) and AR(2) coefficients at $K = 1$ and $K = 5$ knots.

Designs 5 and 6 are evaluated under both protocols of \sref{sec:sim-scoring}.  The spatial hold-out evaluation fits the same nine models as the other designs; the time-block forecast evaluation fits the three $K = 1$ \skewt variants whose latent processes are i.i.d.\ in time, AR(1), and AR(2), so that the value of the second lag is read off directly as the AR(2) minus AR(1) contrast.  The fixed-AR(2) design with $(\phi_1, \phi_2) = (0.80, -0.35)$ joins them as a short-memory control, fit by the same three variants.  Its autocorrelation decays within six days, well inside the $15$-day forecast horizon, so a comparison that reports a gain there would be reporting the extra parameters rather than the memory.

The forecast fits differ from the rest of the study in one respect, and for an identifiability reason.  The mean of \eref{eq:fullmodel} involves $\lambda$ and $|z_t(\bs)|$ only through their product, so the data identify the pair only up to a positive scale factor, and it is the half-normal law on $z$ that pins the scale down.  On the short prefixes that a forecast origin leaves, that law is weakly informative, and a chain can settle on a reflected branch in which $\lambda$ turns negative and $\beta_0$ rises so that the fitted mean is unchanged, while the predictive standard deviation grows in proportion to $|\lambda|$.  The forecast fits therefore restrict the support, $\lambda \sim \mathrm{HN}(0, 20)$, which fixes the direction of the skewness without constraining its magnitude and is drawn exactly by a truncated Gibbs step.  Right skewness is the case of interest both in these designs, generated at $\lambda = 3$, and in the ozone data of \sref{sec:ozone-data-desc}, so fixing the sign discards no case that the evaluation needs.  The restriction is confined to the time-block forecast fits of this experiment.  Every other fit reported in this thesis keeps the unrestricted $N(0, 20)$ prior of \eref{eq:stage3-priors}, including the spatial hold-out fits of this experiment, those of \sref{sec:sim-mrts-design}, and the ozone fits of \sref{sec:ozonedata}.  The reflected branch is a property of the model rather than of the forecast protocol, so it can arise under the unrestricted prior as well.  What differs is the information available to rule it out.  A spatial hold-out fit sees all $50$ days at $100$ sites, so the scale of $z$ is far better determined there than on the prefix a forecast origin leaves, and the restriction is needed only where that information is thin.

When the day being predicted is already observed at the training sites, that day's cross-sectional information dominates and the temporal prior has little left to add.

The time-block forecast evaluation answers the question the site split cannot, and it answers it differently for each generating design, according to whether the generating memory reaches past the forecast horizon in a form the AR(2) prior can represent (\fref{fig:bs-lead-timeblock}, Table~\ref{tab:timeblock-brier}).  Under $(\phi_1, \phi_2) = (0.15, 0.80)$ the AR(2) fit improves on both competitors at both thresholds, at both lead windows, and at every individual lead.  Pooled over the full horizon its mean Brier score is $0.0949$ against $0.1207$ for the i.i.d.\ fit at the $0.90$ quantile, a reduction of $21\%$ with a paired interval of $[-42.5,\, -9.1] \times 10^{-3}$; at the $0.95$ quantile the reduction is $13\%$ with the interval $[-14.2,\, -3.6] \times 10^{-3}$.  What pays there is the second lag rather than temporal pooling as such.  The AR(1) fit is indistinguishable from the i.i.d.\ one over the full horizon, at $+1.6 \times 10^{-3}$ with an interval covering zero, whereas AR(2) minus AR(1) is $-10.5 \times 10^{-3}$ with the interval $[-16.0,\, -5.0] \times 10^{-3}$, and that contrast separates at every threshold from $0.90$ to $0.98$.  The short-memory control fixes the scale of the claim.  Its autocorrelation decays within six days, so over a fifteen-day horizon nothing should be detectable, and nothing is.  The pooled AR(2) minus i.i.d.\ difference is $-0.00 \times 10^{-3}$ at the $0.90$ quantile and $-0.08 \times 10^{-3}$ at the $0.95$, both intervals covering zero, and the same holds at every one of the eleven thresholds.  One design in which the extra lag is worth nothing returns nothing, and one in which it is worth a great deal returns a large and well separated gain, under a single protocol and a single estimator.

Under long memory no contrast separates from zero.  No interval, at either threshold or either lead window, excludes zero, and no difference in the block exceeds $2.1 \times 10^{-3}$ in magnitude.  A calibration check of the predictive tail locates the reason.  Alongside each Brier score we record the predicted exceedance probability and the realized exceedance frequency at the same level, both computed from the forecasts and the thresholds that enter Table~\ref{tab:timeblock-brier} and both averaged over the held-out sites, the fifteen leads, the five forecast windows and the ten data sets.  Under long memory the AR(2) predictive over-predicts exceedance at every level of the eleven-threshold grid, and the ratio of the predicted rate to the realized one rises monotonically with the level.  It runs from $1.13$ at the $0.90$ quantile, where $0.1250$ is predicted against a realized $0.1111$, to $4.11$ at the $0.995$ quantile, the deepest level of the grid, where $0.0234$ is predicted against a realized $0.0057$.  The fitted AR(2) therefore misplaces the upper-tail level of its predictive distribution under hyperbolic decay, and a temporal structure that is fitted but mislevelled cannot convert into skill at a threshold.  The mean scores are ordered consistently with that reading.  Over the full horizon the AR(1) fit carries the highest mean Brier score at both thresholds, so a single geometric lag is the least appropriate summary of a hyperbolically decaying memory.  The three fits nevertheless lie within $2.7 \times 10^{-3}$ of one another, inside the width of every interval in the block, so that ordering is descriptive and no claim rests on it.

Of the $24$ intervals in Table~\ref{tab:timeblock-brier}, $8$ exclude zero and all $8$ belong to the $(0.15, 0.80)$ design.  The smallest two-sided $p$-value is $0.0004$, which survives a Bonferroni correction across all $24$ comparisons at $0.05/24 = 0.002$, so that result is not an artifact of multiplicity.  The two-sided Wilcoxon $p$-values, ranging from $0.002$ to $1.00$, agree with the intervals throughout.  Read beside the spatial hold-out evaluation, which is null under the same generating designs and the same models, the contrast is a property of the prediction task.  Temporal structure is worth little when the day in question is observed elsewhere, and worth a great deal when it is not.

\subsection{The MRTS experiment}\label{sec:sim-mrts-design}

\paragraph{Design.}
This experiment enriches the regression component with the MRTS basis functions $f_1(\bs), \ldots, f_{K_{\mathrm{MRTS}}}(\bs)$ of \sref{sec:meth-mrts}.  The four generating designs place the non-stationarity first where the mean basis cannot reach it (designs 1--2) and then where it can (designs 3--4):
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=3em]
  \item \Skewt, $K = 5$ knots, $\lambda = 3$, with the constant mean $\bX^\top \bbeta = 10$;
  \item \Skewt, $K = 1$ knot, $\lambda = 3$, with an additive mean-zero non-stationary spatial random effect $u_t(\bs)$, redrawn daily from two fixed cosine fields (Appendix~\ref{app:nsdep-cov}), so the non-stationarity lies in the second moment while the marginal mean stays at $10$;
  \item Gaussian, sigmoid front: the nonlinear-mean model \eref{eq:nont-gen} with $\tilde g(\bs) = \tanh\{\lVert \bs - (2, 2)^\top \rVert - 4.5\}$, a smoothed circular step;
  \item Gaussian, transform mix: the nonlinear-mean model \eref{eq:nont-gen} with $\tilde g(\bs) = \mathrm{std}\{\log(1 + s_1)\} + \mathrm{std}\{e^{-s_1/2}\} + \mathrm{std}\{s_1 / (1 + s_2)\}$, where $\mathrm{std}\{\cdot\}$ standardizes each term to unit spatial standard deviation.  The curvature of this surface concentrates near the domain boundary, where the uniform site draw is sparsest.
\end{enumerate}

The nonlinear-mean designs replace the \skewt generator with Gaussian errors,
\begin{align}
  Y_t(\bs) \;=\; 10 \;+\; g(\bs) \;+\; \sigma_g\, \varepsilon_t(\bs), \qquad \sigma_g = 4,
  \label{eq:nont-gen}
\end{align}
where the errors $\varepsilon_t$ are standard Gaussian processes with the \Matern correlation \eref{eq:matern} at the generating values of \sref{sec:sim-design}, drawn independently across days; every $t$-family analysis model is therefore misspecified in its error law.  Each raw surface $\tilde g$ is orthogonalized against the baseline design $[1, s_1, s_2]$ and standardized to a spatial standard deviation of $11$, so the $K_{\mathrm{MRTS}} = 0$ design spans none of the resulting $g$.

Each of the five baseline models is fit across a grid of MRTS basis sizes, $K_{\mathrm{MRTS}} \in \{0, 3, 5, 8, 10, 12, 15, 20, 25\}$, where $K_{\mathrm{MRTS}} = 0$ recovers the no-MRTS baseline.  On both nonlinear-mean designs the grid is extended by $\{30, 40, 50\}$, since the finer surfaces are resolved only by a fine basis.

\paragraph{Results.}\label{sec:sim-mrts-results}
On the two \skewt-core designs, augmenting the regression component with MRTS basis functions does not deliver a stable improvement in upper-tail scores, and enlarging the basis does not lower the average Brier score on either setting (\fref{fig:bs-mean-vs-K-mrts}).  On the five-knot setting every curve is essentially flat in $K_{\mathrm{MRTS}}$; on the additive non-stationary random-effect process the curves are likewise flat, with over-specified models destabilizing at large $K_{\mathrm{MRTS}}$.  As in the AR(2) experiment, the generating knot count dominates the ranking.  The second setting is the decisive one.  The non-stationarity lives in the second moment (a daily-redrawn random effect $u_t$), whereas the MRTS basis enters only the fixed-effect mean.  Because $u_t$ has zero time-average, the time-pooled coefficient $\bbeta^*$ has no first-moment structure to recover, so the Brier curves stay flat by construction rather than through absence of signal, since $u_t$ contributes substantial (spatially non-stationary) variance.  The MRTS mean term therefore yields no reliable tail-score gain.  A fixed-effect mean cannot represent a non-stationary second-moment feature, and so, in these designs, the spatial mean is not the binding constraint on upper-tail prediction.

The two nonlinear-mean designs address the complementary case (\fref{fig:bs-mean-vs-K-nont}; Table~\ref{tab:mrts-nont-brier}).  On the sigmoid-front design the Brier score finally responds to the mean basis, but only once the basis is fine enough to resolve the front.  The profiles stay flat for a coarse basis, deteriorate slightly at intermediate sizes, and improve only at the finer bases, with the gains larger at the higher exceedance level.  The deterioration at intermediate sizes has a single cause.  A partially fitted mean sharpens the predictive distribution while leaving a residual surface error that carries over to the held-out sites, and the Brier score worsens until the basis actually resolves the front.

The transform-mix design shows the same mechanism operating from a much worse starting point.  The mismatched five-knot partition, compounded for the thresholded variant by censoring, leaves the no-basis baselines of the five-knot models far above those of the one-knot models, and the mean basis recovers most of that loss.  Every model improves at the $0.95$ threshold quantile, and at its best basis size each converges to nearly the same score.  The improvement is not uniform across levels, however.  At the rarer $0.98$ quantile it is carried entirely by the five-knot models, whereas the one-knot models stay flat or worsen.  This is the sharpness/residual-error signature again, in that only the models whose no-basis fit is badly misspecified, here the five-knot partition, carry enough headroom for the mean basis to convert into a gain at the rarest level.  Both surfaces leave genuine Brier headroom.  Replacing the fitted mean by the true surface $g$ under the true Gaussian error law improves the score substantially on both.  The realized gains convert only a small part of that headroom, which is consistent with the sharpness/residual-error account above.  The Brier score improves only where the residual basis error is small relative to the predictive spread.

\subsection{Summary of the simulation study}\label{sec:sim-summary}

Overall, the simulation study indicates that both extensions enlarge the usable model class but that neither uniformly dominates the STP baseline.  The AR(2) experiment sharpens this conclusion into a statement about the prediction task itself.  Even under the most persistent generating design, $(\phi_1, \phi_2) = (0.15, 0.80)$, where the temporal coefficients are recovered closely, prediction at withheld sites is insensitive to the autoregressive order, and the same holds under long memory.  The gains from richer temporal pooling appear only once the task is to forecast days beyond the end of the record, and there they turn on whether the generating memory reaches past the forecast horizon in a form the AR(2) prior can represent.  Under $(\phi_1, \phi_2) = (0.15, 0.80)$ it does, and the i.i.d.\ score falls by $13$ to $21\%$ depending on the threshold.  Under $(0.80, -0.35)$ the autocorrelation decays inside the horizon and there is nothing left to gain, while under hyperbolic decay the fitted AR(2) misplaces the upper tail of its predictive distribution and no gain appears either.  Whatever promise the AR(2) extension retains therefore lies in forecasting rather than in prediction at withheld sites.  The MRTS experiments admit an equally structural summary.  The mean term is inert when the non-stationarity lives in the second moment, and under nonlinear mean structure it pays off only once the basis is fine enough to resolve the surface, converting a small fraction of the oracle headroom.  Whether the same pattern holds on real environmental data is the question taken up in \sref{sec:ozonedata}, where the AR(2) and MRTS variants enter as candidate options to be selected by held-out predictive scores rather than as default replacements for the AR(1) STP baseline.

\section{Ozone data analysis}\label{sec:ozonedata}

\textcite{morris_space-time_2017} introduced the spatio-temporal \skewt (STP) process to predict high-threshold ground-level ozone exceedances from U.S. EPA monitoring data.  We return to the same July 2005 data, asking not whether a new model class is needed but whether enlarging the STP candidate class along the two axes of this thesis, the AR(2) temporal prior and the MRTS spatial-mean term, yields better held-out tail prediction.  The comparison is therefore designed to locate where in the upper tail, if anywhere, the enlarged candidate class holds its place among the leading models, rather than to select a single overall-best model.

\subsection{Data}\label{sec:ozone-data-desc}

The response is the daily maximum 8-hour ozone concentration for the $31$ days of July 2005 at U.S. EPA Air Quality System (AQS) monitoring sites, with the co-located Community Multi-scale Air Quality (CMAQ) model output as the sole covariate.  \fref{fig:ozone-maps} summarizes the two data sources through the fourth-highest daily maximum 8-hour average of the month, the within-month analogue of the EPA design value.  The AQS monitors are irregularly spaced, covering the eastern United States and California densely but leaving large gaps in the interior west, whereas CMAQ provides complete gridded coverage of the conterminous United States.  CMAQ reproduces the broad spatial pattern of the observations but not their level at individual monitors, which motivates using it as a regression covariate to be calibrated rather than as a substitute for the observations.  Following \textcite{morris_space-time_2017}, the regression component is $\bX_t(\bs) = [1,\text{CMAQ}_t(\bs)]^\top$, so that each model acts as a statistical calibration of CMAQ against the monitor observations.

\subsection{Candidate models and validation design}\label{sec:ozone-models}

The baseline candidate set follows that of \textcite{morris_space-time_2017}.  We fit Gaussian and \skewt marginals over a range of partition sizes, $K \in \{1,5,6,7,8,9,10,15\}$, and censor the response at $T=0$, at $T=50$ ppb (the $0.42$ sample quantile), and at $T=75$ ppb (the $0.92$ sample quantile).  For settings censored at $T=75$ ppb, we use a symmetric-$t$ marginal, as in the simulation study.  Each setting is fit both with and without a latent time series.  

On top of this baseline we add the two extensions developed in this thesis.  The first replaces the AR(1) latent dynamics with the standardized AR(2) prior of \sref{sec:ar2}; the second augments the regression component with MRTS basis functions, so that non-stationary spatial mean structure can be absorbed by multi-resolution thin-plate splines.  Unlike the simulation study, which sweeps a grid of basis sizes, the ozone candidate set fixes $K_{\mathrm{MRTS}} = 10$, so the MRTS comparison below is a pre-specified one rather than a best-over-$K_{\mathrm{MRTS}}$ selection.

Of the $1{,}089$ sites of \fref{fig:ozone-maps}, the comparison uses a stratified sample of $800$, drawn to represent each region of the United States equally so that the interior west is not swamped by the dense eastern network.  Models are evaluated by two-fold cross validation, with the $800$ sites split at random into $400$ training and $400$ validation sites per fold, scored by the held-out Brier score of \sref{sec:sim-scoring}; robustness of the ranking to the split is examined with two further splits in \sref{sec:ozone-results}.  Both further splits draw on the same $800$ sites, re-partitioned at random, and each is a single training and validation split rather than a pair of folds.  One holds $300$ training and $100$ validation sites and the other $200$ of each, so the remaining $400$ sites of each partition are set aside and enter no score.  Each MCMC chain is run for $30{,}000$ iterations with a burn-in period of $25{,}000$ iterations.

The pattern is at least orderly.  The MRTS extension leads at the $0.900$ level, where the spatial mean still carries predictive signal; the AR(2) extension occupies both top ranks at $0.950$ and returns at the deepest level, $0.995$, where the best settings also shift toward high censoring and large partition sizes; the intermediate levels, $0.980$ and $0.990$, belong to the baseline family.  At the $0.900$ level the two leading settings are the MRTS fit and the otherwise identical fit without the basis, so the lead there is over the extension's own matched baseline rather than over an unrelated model. This picture is reproducible on smaller training samples.  Repeating the comparison under two further train/validation splits, written $N_{\text{train}}/N_{\text{test}}$ for $N_{\text{train}}$ training and $N_{\text{test}}$ validation sites (the 300/100 and 200/200 splits; Tables~\ref{tab:ozone-top4-quantile-300-100} and~\ref{tab:ozone-top4-quantile-200-200}), recovers the same qualitative ranking. That shifting is expected, since only a handful of validation sites exceed the deepest level.  Behind the $0.995$ score stand $120$ exceedance events in the main split, $27$ in the $200/200$ split and $2$ in the $300/100$ split, so the fine ranking there is sensitive to the held-out draw.

What the ranking establishes should be read for what it is.  The two leading scores at each level differ by between $3 \times 10^{-6}$ and $8.3 \times 10^{-5}$.  A single month of a single pollutant supplies one data set, so the replicate layer of \sref{sec:sim-uq} has no counterpart here.  Uncertainty can still be attached at the site level.  We resample the validation sites in clusters ($B = 2000$) and form the paired difference for each extension against its matched baseline, the AR(2) fit against its AR(1) counterpart and the MRTS fit against the same setting without the basis.  Every AR(2) interval covers zero, on all three splits and at all five levels, and the sign of the difference changes from level to level.  The MRTS differences are negative at every level of the two splits on which that pair was run, and three of them exclude zero, at the $0.950$ and $0.990$ levels of the main split and at the $0.995$ level of the $200/200$ split, with two-sided bootstrap $p$-values of $0.011$, $0.020$ and $0.018$.  None of the three survives a correction for the ten comparisons that pair contributes, and no difference anywhere exceeds $1.5 \times 10^{-4}$.  No extension is therefore established as the better predictor at any single level, though the consistent sign of the MRTS differences is what a small real gain would look like.  What does carry across the tail, and across all three splits, is that both extensions hold their place among the leading models rather than falling behind it.  The case for retaining them accordingly rests on the standing of the candidate class across splits and levels rather than on the size of any single gain.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

This thesis asked not whether the spatio-temporal \skewt process predicts
high-threshold exceedances well, which \textcite{morris_space-time_2017} already
established, but where within it room for sharper upper-tail prediction
remains.

The temporal axis divides along the prediction task.  Added memory leaves
prediction at withheld sites unchanged, and not for want of signal.  Under
$(\phi_1, \phi_2) = (0.15, 0.80)$ the coefficients were recovered closely, and
under long memory the fitted persistence was substantial.  In both the held-out
scores still did not move, because with the day in question observed at the
training sites the cross-sectional information dominates.  Forecasting days
beyond the end of the record is where the memory paid, and what decided the
payment was whether the generating memory reached past the horizon in a form
the AR(2) prior could represent.  It was nil where the autocorrelation decayed
inside the forecast horizon, nil again under hyperbolic decay, where the fitted
AR(2) misplaced the upper tail of its predictive distribution, and large under
$(\phi_1, \phi_2) = (0.15, 0.80)$, where the mean Brier score fell by $13$ to
$21\%$ against the i.i.d.\ fit and the whole of that gain was carried by the
second lag rather than the first.

The spatial axis pays where the non-stationarity sits in the mean.  A
fixed-effect basis reaches first-moment structure and nothing else, which is
why the MRTS term is inert against a non-stationary second moment.  Where the
mean surface is nonlinear the term does improve the score, once the basis is
fine enough to resolve that surface, and it converts a small part of the
headroom the true surface shows to be available.  Where it converts most, it is
repairing a misspecified partition rather than adding to a fit that is already
well specified.  On the ozone data neither extension separates from its
matched baseline.

The implication extends beyond ozone, since the same target, a spatial
probability statement about the upper tail at unmonitored sites, arises for any
heavy-tailed, asymmetric environmental field regulated by a high threshold.
Temporal order and mean flexibility are not free improvements to such a model,
but neither are they idle.  Rather than replacing the STP baseline, the
standardized AR(2) prior and the MRTS mean enlarge it into a candidate class
from which held-out tail scores can select the setting that fits the data at
hand.  Where each axis fell short marks where to go next.  Entering the MRTS
basis with random coefficients, as in fixed-rank kriging, would place the
flexibility where the simulation showed the structure to be.  A
parameterization of $z$ that removes the scale ridge of
\sref{sec:sim-ar2-design}, together with longer records, would test whether the
forecast gains survive at horizons beyond the fifteen days examined here, and
whether they appear on observed rather than simulated dynamics.

\section{Stationary moments of the standardized AR(2)}\label{app:ar2-moments}

We derive the Yule--Walker moments \eref{eq:gamma12}--\eref{eq:sigeps} used to standardize the AR(2) prior \eref{eq:ar2-recursion} to unit marginal variance.  Let $\gamma_h = \mathrm{Cov}(Y_t, Y_{t-h})$ be the stationary autocovariances, constrained so that $\gamma_0 = \mathrm{Var}(Y_t) = 1$.  Multiplying the recursion \eref{eq:ar2-recursion} by $Y_{t-h}$ for $h \ge 1$ and taking expectations, and using that $\epsilon_t$ is independent of $Y_{t-h}$ for the causal stationary solution, yields the Yule--Walker recursion
\begin{align}
  \gamma_h \;=\; \phi_1\, \gamma_{h-1} \;+\; \phi_2\, \gamma_{h-2}, \qquad h \ge 1. \label{eq:yw}
\end{align}
Evaluating \eref{eq:yw} at $h = 1$ and using the symmetry $\gamma_{-1} = \gamma_1$ with $\gamma_0 = 1$ gives $\gamma_1 = \phi_1 + \phi_2\, \gamma_1$, hence $\gamma_1 = \phi_1 / (1 - \phi_2)$, and at $h = 2$ gives $\gamma_2 = \phi_1\, \gamma_1 + \phi_2$; together these are \eref{eq:gamma12}.  Multiplying \eref{eq:ar2-recursion} instead by $Y_t$ and taking expectations, with $\mathrm{E}[Y_t\, \epsilon_t] = \sigma_\epsilon^2$ because $Y_t$ carries the current innovation, gives $\gamma_0 = \phi_1\, \gamma_1 + \phi_2\, \gamma_2 + \sigma_\epsilon^2$, and solving for $\sigma_\epsilon^2$ under $\gamma_0 = 1$ recovers \eref{eq:sigeps}.  The same normalization fixes the initial pair, which has unit variances and lag-1 covariance $\gamma_1$ and is therefore the stationary bivariate Gaussian \eref{eq:joint-init}.

\section{AR(2) full conditionals for the Metropolis-within-Gibbs update}\label{app:full-cond}

\paragraph{Updating the time-varying parameters.}
The AR(2) Markov structure factorizes the joint prior of $(Y_1, \ldots, Y_{n_t})$ as
\begin{align}
  p(Y_1, \ldots, Y_{n_t})
  \;=\; p(Y_1, Y_2) \prod_{s = 3}^{n_t} p(Y_s \mid Y_{s-1}, Y_{s-2}), \label{eq:joint-prior}
\end{align}
with $p(Y_1, Y_2)$ given by \eref{eq:joint-init} and each conditional factor for $s \ge 3$ following \eref{eq:ar2-recursion}.  For an interior time point $3 \le t \le n_t - 2$, the value $Y_t$ enters \eref{eq:joint-prior} through three factors, namely the self factor $p(Y_t \mid Y_{t-1}, Y_{t-2})$, the lag-1 factor $p(Y_{t+1} \mid Y_t, Y_{t-1})$, and the lag-2 factor $p(Y_{t+2} \mid Y_{t+1}, Y_t)$.  The full conditional targeted by the Metropolis step for $Y_t$ is therefore
\begin{align}
  \pi(Y_t \mid Y_{-t}, \bY)
  \;\propto\;
  L(\bY \mid Y_t) \,\cdot\,
  \underbrace{p(Y_t \mid Y_{t-1}, Y_{t-2})}_{\text{self}}
  \,\cdot\,
  \underbrace{p(Y_{t+1} \mid Y_t, Y_{t-1})}_{\text{lag-1}}
  \,\cdot\,
  \underbrace{p(Y_{t+2} \mid Y_{t+1}, Y_t)}_{\text{lag-2}}, \label{eq:full-cond}
\end{align}
where $L(\bY \mid Y_t)$ is the spatial likelihood contributed by $Y_t$ through the partition and Gaussian process structure of \eref{eq:fullmodel}.  The lag-2 factor is absent for $t = n_t - 1$ and both lag-1 and lag-2 factors are absent for $t = n_t$.  At the head of the series the initial-pair density \eref{eq:joint-init} plays the role of the self factor.  Accordingly, $Y_1$ enters only the initial-pair factor $p(Y_1, Y_2)$ and the conditional $p(Y_3 \mid Y_2, Y_1)$, while $Y_2$ enters $p(Y_1, Y_2)$, $p(Y_3 \mid Y_2, Y_1)$, and $p(Y_4 \mid Y_3, Y_2)$; in each case the factors referencing a non-existent time index are simply dropped.  Under a symmetric Gaussian random-walk proposal $Y_t^{(c)} \sim N(Y_t, s^2)$, the Metropolis--Hastings acceptance ratio is
\begin{align}
  R \;=\; \min\!\left(1,\; \frac{\pi(Y_t^{(c)} \mid Y_{-t}, \bY)}{\pi(Y_t \mid Y_{-t}, \bY)}\right), \label{eq:mh-ratio}
\end{align}
in which all three prior factors must appear; omitting the lag-2 factor distorts the stationary distribution of the chain away from the AR(2) joint and produces a lag-2 autocorrelation that does not match the Yule--Walker value in \eref{eq:gamma12}.  The proposal scale is adapted during the first half of the burn-in period, by factors $0.8$ and $1.2$ whenever the running acceptance rate leaves $[0.25, 0.50]$, and is held fixed thereafter, so that the sampling phase runs a time-homogeneous kernel.  The move is componentwise for $z^*$ and $\sigma^{2*}$, while for the knot locations the $2K$ coordinates of a given day are proposed and accepted as a single block, which leaves \eref{eq:mh-ratio} unchanged in form because the block proposal is again a symmetric random walk.

\paragraph{Updating the autoregressive coefficients.}
Restoring the knot index, write $Y_{tk}$ for the transformed series of knot $k$ and $N(x;\, \mu,\, \sigma^2)$ for the normal density evaluated at $x$.  Conditional on those series, $\bphi^{(\cdot)}$ is independent of the data and of every other parameter, so its full conditional carries only the AR(2) prior and the coefficient prior of \eref{eq:stage3-priors},
\begin{align}
  \pi\big(\bphi^{(\cdot)} \mid Y\big)
  \;\propto\;
  \Bigg[ \prod_{k=1}^{K} p\big(Y_{1k}, \ldots, Y_{n_t k} \mid \bphi^{(\cdot)}\big) \Bigg]\,
  N_2\big(\bphi^{(\cdot)};\, \bZero,\, \sigma^2_\phi \bI_2\big)\,
  I\big(\bphi^{(\cdot)} \in \calS\big),
  \label{eq:phi-full-cond}
\end{align}
the product running over the $K$ knots, which are independent replicates of the same recursion, and over both coordinates in the case of $\bw^*$.  Expanding each factor by \eref{eq:joint-prior} and discarding $p(Y_{1k}) = N(0, 1)$, which does not involve $\bphi^{(\cdot)}$, the log-likelihood in \eref{eq:phi-full-cond} is
\begin{align}
  \ell\big(\bphi^{(\cdot)}\big)
  \;=\;
  \sum_{k=1}^{K} \log N\big(Y_{2k};\; \gamma_1 Y_{1k},\; 1 - \gamma_1^2\big)
  \;+\;
  \sum_{k=1}^{K} \sum_{t=3}^{n_t}
    \log N\big(Y_{tk};\; \phi_1 Y_{t-1,k} + \phi_2 Y_{t-2,k},\; \sigma_\epsilon^2\big),
  \label{eq:phi-loglik}
\end{align}
with $\gamma_1$ and $\sigma_\epsilon^2$ the functions of $\bphi^{(\cdot)}$ fixed by \eref{eq:gamma12}--\eref{eq:sigeps}.  The first sum is the contribution of the initial pair and cannot be dropped, because it depends on the coefficients through $\gamma_1 = \phi_1 / (1 - \phi_2)$.  Keeping only the transitions would leave the coefficient update targeting the conditional likelihood while the update of $Y_t$ above targets the full joint, so that the two blocks of the sampler no longer share a stationary distribution.  Under AR(1) the same omission is harmless, since there the only discarded factor is $p(Y_1) = N(0, 1)$ and the day-$2$ factor is itself a transition.

The two coefficients are drawn one at a time, the second conditioning on the value of the first just accepted.  For $\phi_1$ a candidate is proposed from the untruncated random walk $\phi_1^{(c)} \sim N(\phi_1, s_\phi^2)$ and, when $(\phi_1^{(c)}, \phi_2) \in \calS$, is accepted with probability
\begin{align}
  \min\!\left(1,\;
    \frac{\exp\{\ell(\phi_1^{(c)}, \phi_2)\}\; N\big(\phi_1^{(c)};\, 0,\, \sigma^2_\phi\big)}
         {\exp\{\ell(\phi_1, \phi_2)\}\; N\big(\phi_1;\, 0,\, \sigma^2_\phi\big)}\right),
  \label{eq:phi-mh-ratio}
\end{align}
while a candidate falling outside $\calS$ is rejected outright and the chain holds at its current value.  The coefficient $\phi_2$ is drawn the same way with the roles of the two coefficients exchanged, and $s_\phi$ is adapted by the rule stated above.  Rejecting outside $\calS$ needs no Hastings adjustment.  The proposal is symmetric and the acceptance region $\calS$ does not depend on the current state.  For any two points of $\calS$ the
proposal density is therefore the same in both directions, detailed balance holds with respect to \eref{eq:phi-full-cond} restricted to $\calS$, and the mass proposed outside $\calS$ only raises the probability of staying put.  A proposal truncated to $\calS$ together with the matching asymmetry correction, the scheme used by the AR(1) baseline, is equally valid but defines a different chain.  One pair $\bphi^{(\cdot)}$ is shared by all $K$ knots of its process.

\section{Marginal covariance of the non-stationary-dependence design}\label{app:nsdep-cov}

The second MRTS design of \sref{sec:sim-mrts-design} places the non-stationarity in the dependence through a mean-zero random effect built from two fixed cosine fields on the $[0,10]^2$ domain.  With centers $\bc_1 = (0, 10)^\top$, $\bc_2 = (7.5, 2.5)^\top$ and wavenumbers $\kappa_1 = \pi/10$, $\kappa_2 = \pi/5$,
\begin{align}
  g_j(\bs) = \cos\!\big(\kappa_j \, \| \bs - \bc_j \|\big), \qquad j = 1, 2,
  \label{eq:nsdep-basis}
\end{align}
and the random effect superposes them with independent, daily-redrawn Gaussian weights,
\begin{align}
  u_t(\bs) = \sum_{j=1}^{2} \xi_{tj}\, g_j(\bs), \qquad \xi_{tj} \iid N(0, \tau_j^2), \quad (\tau_1, \tau_2) = (5, 3),
  \label{eq:nsdep-re}
\end{align}
independently across $j$ and across days $t$.  The response adds $u_t$ to the one-knot \skewt model,
\begin{align}
  Y_t(\bs) = 10 + \lambda \, \sigma_t(\bs) \, |z_t(\bs)| + \sigma_t(\bs) \, v_t(\bs) + u_t(\bs).
  \label{eq:nsdep-gen}
\end{align}
Marginalizing over the weights, $u_t(\bs) = \sum_{j} \xi_{tj}\, g_j(\bs)$ has zero mean, $\mathrm{E}[u_t(\bs)] = \sum_j g_j(\bs)\, \mathrm{E}[\xi_{tj}] = 0$, and, by the independence of $\xi_{t1}$ and $\xi_{t2}$, the low-rank covariance
\begin{align}
  \mathrm{Cov}\big(u_t(\bs), u_t(\bs')\big)
    \;=\; \sum_{j=1}^{2} \tau_j^2\, g_j(\bs)\, g_j(\bs').
  \label{eq:nsdep-cov}
\end{align}
The pointwise variance $\mathrm{Var}\big(u_t(\bs)\big) = \sum_{j} \tau_j^2\, g_j(\bs)^2$ depends on location, so the dependence is non-stationary while the marginal mean stays constant at $10$.  Because the weights are redrawn independently across days, $u_t$ carries no temporal dependence, keeping this design orthogonal to the AR(2) experiment.
