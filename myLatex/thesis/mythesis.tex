\hspace*{1.5em}在環境風險治理中，決策關心的通常不是平均濃度，而是未設站位置超越高門檻的機率。本文以 Morris et al. (2017) 的時空 \skewt 過程 (STP) 為基準，沿兩軸檢驗其可改進空間：潛在參數的標準化 AR(2) 先驗，與迴歸均值中的多尺度薄板樣條 (MRTS) 基底。模型比較以樣本外 Brier 分數為核心指標，於模擬與 2005 年 7 月美國 AQS 臭氧資料（CMAQ 共變數）上進行。時間軸的結果依預測任務而分：即使係數被完整回收，空間內插仍對自迴歸階數不敏感；只有當任務需要外推至觀測紀錄之外，較豐富的時間 pooling 才帶來從未顯著的增益。MRTS 均值項在非定態確實位於均值曲面、且基底足以解析它時可改善預測；於臭氧資料上其增益真實，惟幅度不大。因此本文的貢獻在於將 STP 基準擴展為可由樣本外門檻預測表現進行資料驅動選擇的候選模型類。

\section*{\centering Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Abstract} % 加入目錄

In environmental risk regulation, the inferential target is often not the field
mean but the probability of exceeding a high threshold at unmonitored
locations. We take the spatio-temporal \skewt process (STP) of Morris et al.
(2017) as a baseline and examine where predictive improvement remains possible
along two axes: a standardized AR(2) prior on the latent parameters and
multi-resolution thin-plate spline (MRTS) basis functions in the regression
mean. Models are compared by held-out Brier scores in simulation and on July
2005 U.S. AQS ozone data with CMAQ covariates. The temporal axis divides along
the prediction task: spatial interpolation is insensitive to the autoregressive
order even where the coefficients are recovered closely, and richer temporal
pooling helps only when the task requires extrapolating beyond the observed
record, by margins never significant. The MRTS mean improves prediction where
the non-stationarity lies in the mean surface rather than the second moment
and the basis is fine enough to resolve it; on the ozone data the gain is
present but modest in magnitude. The contribution is therefore to enlarge the
STP baseline into a candidate model class selected by held-out threshold
predictive performance.

\vspace{2.0\baselineskip}

\noindent \textbf{Keywords}: spatio-temporal \skewt process; threshold exceedance prediction; AR(2) prior; multi-resolution thin-plate splines; Brier score; ozone

\section{Introduction}
\pagenumbering{arabic}                  % 阿拉伯數字
\setcounter{page}{1}                    % 將頁碼重設為 1
\WatermarkOn                            % 浮水印

Demand for accurate pollution-concentration estimates over broad spatial
domains continues to grow, driven by epidemiological evidence tying air quality
to morbidity and mortality and by tightening regulation, yet the cost and
logistics of a monitoring network cap the coverage that direct measurement can
provide. This gap motivates statistical methods
that extend a network's spatial reach with quantified confidence
\parencite{epa2024downscaler}. Ground-level ozone is the canonical regulatory
case. The National Ambient Air Quality Standard (NAAQS) specifies the maximum
concentration of ozone (O$_3$) allowed in outdoor air.  A site complies only if
the three-year average of its annual fourth-highest daily maximum eight-hour
concentration stays at or below the current NAAQS of $0.070$ parts per million
($70$ ppb), last revised in 2015. The Clean Air Act requires the EPA to review
the standard at least every five years; Table~\ref{tab:ozone-standards} lists
its successive revisions.
The policy-relevant target is thus not the mean field but its upper tail,
namely the probability that an unmonitored location breaches a high threshold;
our goal is to predict that probability at unmonitored sites and to map it
across the domain.

A spatial model for ozone threshold exceedances warrants special consideration,
since standard spatial methods are likely to perform poorly. Two features of the
problem are decisive. First, likelihood-based spatial modeling typically assumes
a Gaussian process, which is appropriate when interest lies in mean behavior. The
Gaussian distribution is light-tailed and symmetric. Daily ozone maxima are
neither. Their residuals are heavy-tailed and are well described by a \skewt
distribution \parencite{morris_space-time_2017}. Second, our interest is in the
dependence structure at high levels, which a covariance designed for mean
behavior does not capture. A Gaussian process is asymptotically independent
outside the degenerate case of perfect dependence, so the probability that two
sites are jointly extreme decays to zero as the level increases. Nearby ozone
monitors, however, tend to record high values on the same days, and this
agreement weakens with distance \parencite{morris_space-time_2017}. These
considerations call for a heavy-tailed, asymmetric margin that retains asymptotic
dependence. The \skewt distribution provides both. It is a subasymptotic model,
capturing the joint tail decay at high but finite levels rather than the limiting
dependence of extreme events \parencite{huser_advances_2022}. This is the setting
the ozone data occupy.

Our approach also departs from threshold models based on extreme-value
distributions, a well-developed part of the field in which extreme events are
defined as exceedances over a high threshold. \textcite{Davison1990} model
univariate exceedances with the generalized Pareto distribution.
\textcite{Ledford1996} address the multivariate case through a censored
likelihood that distinguishes the ways in which a threshold may be exceeded.
\textcite{Wadsworth2012} and \textcite{Thibaud2013} carried these ideas to
spatial extremes, fitting models by a censored pairwise likelihood
\parencite{Padoan2010}. \textcite{Huser2014} extended them to space-time data.
Such methods are grounded in extreme-value theory and presume a threshold high
enough for extremal models to be valid and for extrapolation beyond the observed
range. They are also computationally demanding and, in practice, restricted to
small datasets. Neither feature suits the present application, since the 75 ppb threshold
corresponds to roughly the 0.92 sample quantile of the daily ozone maxima
\parencite{morris_space-time_2017}, well short of the far tail that extremal
models require, and the network, at $1{,}089$ monitoring stations, is far larger
than such methods can handle. A complementary strategy retains the max-stable process while
reducing its computational cost. Fully Bayesian inference for max-stable
processes is cumbersome at scale. \textcite{morris_exploration_2019} address this
by truncating the spectral representation to a small set of empirical basis
functions that carry the spatial dependence. Their construction renders inference
feasible for large datasets and, as a by-product, dispenses with the assumption
of stationarity.

In this thesis we build on the spatial \skewt process of
\textcite{morris_space-time_2017}. This process is not max-stable, yet it retains
extremal dependence. Their model has four components. A \skewt margin, built by
location-scale mixing of Gaussian processes, supplies heavy tails and asymmetry.
A random spatial partition, redrawn each day,
confines asymptotic dependence to nearby sites. A censored likelihood focuses
inference on exceedances above a threshold. A transformed first-order
autoregressive process carries dependence through time. We extend this baseline
along two axes. On the temporal axis, the autoregressive prior links each day
only to the one before, and we add a further lag to capture longer-range
dependence in the record. On the spatial axis, the baseline assumes a stationary
\Matern covariance, restrictive over a continental network.
We therefore introduce the multi-resolution thin-plate spline basis of
\textcite{tzeng_resolution_2018} as fixed-effect covariates in the mean,
following the basis-function idea reviewed above, so as to relax stationarity at
little additional cost. Each axis is a candidate extension rather than a
replacement of the baseline, and together they are intended to improve prediction
of threshold exceedances.

The remainder of the thesis is organized as follows. \sref{sec:methodology}
reviews the spatial \skewt process of \textcite{morris_space-time_2017}, develops
the two extensions in turn (the standardized AR(2) prior on the latent dynamics
and the MRTS basis functions in the mean), and assembles them into a single
hierarchical model together with the MCMC algorithm used for inference.
\sref{sec:simulation} evaluates each extension on synthetic data, describing the
shared simulation design and the validation protocols, with the uncertainty
attached to each score comparison, and reporting the AR(2) and MRTS experiments
in turn.
\sref{sec:ozonedata} applies the models to the ozone monitoring data, comparing the
baseline against the two extensions in their ability to predict threshold
exceedances. \sref{sec:discussion} concludes and outlines directions for further
work. Technical derivations and design details are collected in the appendix.

\section{Methodology}\label{sec:methodology}

This section reviews the spatio-temporal skew-$t$ process (STP) of \textcite{morris_space-time_2017}, develops the AR(2) prior on the Gaussian-copula margins of its latent processes and the multi-resolution thin-plate spline (MRTS) augmentation of its regression mean, and assembles the full hierarchical model with the MCMC algorithm used for inference.

\subsection{The spatio-temporal skew-$t$ model}\label{sec:sttskewt}

Let $Y_t(\bs)$ denote the response at spatial location $\bs \in \calD \subset \calR^2$ on day $t \in \{1, \ldots, n_t\}$.  Following \textcite{morris_space-time_2017}, we model
\begin{align}
  Y_t(\bs) \;=\; \bX_t(\bs)^\top \bbeta \;+\; \lambda\, \sigma_t(\bs)\, |z_t(\bs)| \;+\; \sigma_t(\bs)\, v_t(\bs), \label{eq:fullmodel}
\end{align}
where $\bX_t(\bs)$ is a $p$-vector of covariates, $\bbeta$ is the corresponding regression coefficient vector, $\lambda \in \calR$ is a scalar skewness parameter, and $v_t(\bs)$ is a standard Gaussian process with stationary isotropic \Matern correlation
\begin{align}
  \rho(h) \;=\; (1 - \eta)\, I(h = 0) \;+\; \eta\, \frac{1}{\Gamma(\nu)\, 2^{\nu - 1}} \left( \sqrt{2\nu}\, \frac{h}{\varphi} \right)^{\nu} K_\nu\!\left( \sqrt{2\nu}\, \frac{h}{\varphi} \right), \label{eq:matern}
\end{align}
where $\varphi > 0$ is the spatial range, $\nu > 0$ is the smoothness, $\eta \in [0, 1]$ is the proportion of variance attributed to the spatial component, $K_\nu$ is the modified Bessel function of the second kind, and $h = \|\bs_1 - \bs_2\|$ is the Euclidean distance between sites $\bs_1$ and $\bs_2$.  Marginalizing over $|z_t(\bs)|$ and $\sigma_t(\bs)$, the finite-dimensional vector $\bY_t = [Y_t(\bs_1), \ldots, Y_t(\bs_n)]^\top$ follows an $n$-dimensional \skewt distribution; consequently \eref{eq:fullmodel} provides asymmetric and heavy-tailed margins together with asymptotic dependence between nearby sites.

\paragraph{Censoring.}
Because the EPA compliance criterion focuses on exceedances of a high level $L$, the model fits only the upper tail by censoring the data at a pre-specified threshold $T \le L$.  Writing the partially censored response $\widetilde{Y}_t(\bs) = \max\{Y_t(\bs), T\}$, inference is based on $\widetilde{\bY}_t = [\widetilde{Y}_t(\bs_1), \ldots, \widetilde{Y}_t(\bs_n)]^\top$, and the censored values below $T$ are imputed inside the MCMC.

\paragraph{Random Partition.}
To remove the long-range asymptotic dependence that arises when a single scalar $z$ and $\sigma^2$ are shared across the entire domain, the model uses a daily random partition.  For each day $t$, let $\bw_{t1}, \ldots, \bw_{tK}$ denote $K$ spatial knots in $\calD$, and define the partition subregions
\begin{align}
  P_{tk} \;=\; \{\bs \in \calD : k = \argmin_\ell \|\bs - \bw_{t\ell}\| \}, \qquad k = 1, \ldots, K.
\end{align}
For $\bs \in P_{tk}$ we set $z_t(\bs) = z_{tk}$ and $\sigma^2_t(\bs) = \sigma^2_{tk}$, with $z_{tk} \iid N(0, 1)$ and $\sigma^2_{tk} \iid \mathrm{IG}(a/2, b/2)$.  Within each subregion the model retains the \skewt form, while sites in different partitions are not asymptotically dependent.

\paragraph{Temporal Dependence.}
Daily temporal dependence is introduced on the latent parameters that drive the tail, namely the knot locations $\bw_{tk}$, the skewness latents $z_{tk}$, and the scale parameters $\sigma^2_{tk}$.  To preserve the marginal \skewt distribution at every day, each parameter is first transformed to a standard normal scale via the probability integral transform (PIT):
\begin{align}
  w^*_{tki} &= \Phi^{-1}\!\left[\frac{w_{tki} - \min(\bs_i)}{\max(\bs_i) - \min(\bs_i)}\right], \quad i = 1, 2, \label{eq:wstar}\\
  z^*_{tk}  &= \Phi^{-1}\{\mathrm{HN}(z_{tk})\}, \label{eq:zstar}\\
  \sigma^{2*}_{tk} &= \Phi^{-1}\{\mathrm{IG}(\sigma^2_{tk})\}, \label{eq:sigmastar}
\end{align}
where $\Phi$ is the standard normal distribution function, $\mathrm{HN}$ is the half-normal distribution function, and $\mathrm{IG}$ is the inverse-gamma distribution function with parameters $(a/2, b/2)$.  After transformation, each $\bw^*_{tk}, z^*_{tk}, \sigma^{2*}_{tk}$ has a standard normal marginal distribution, and \textcite{morris_space-time_2017} place a stationary AR(1) time series on each of them:
\begin{align}
  \bw^*_{tk} \mid \bw^*_{t-1, k} &\sim N_2\!\left(\phi^{(w)}\, \bw^*_{t-1, k},\; (1 - (\phi^{(w)})^2)\, \bI_2\right), \label{eq:ar1-w}\\
  z^*_{tk} \mid z^*_{t-1, k} &\sim N\!\left(\phi^{(z)}\, z^*_{t-1, k},\; 1 - (\phi^{(z)})^2\right), \label{eq:ar1-z}\\
  \sigma^{2*}_{tk} \mid \sigma^{2*}_{t-1, k} &\sim N\!\left(\phi^{(\sigma)}\, \sigma^{2*}_{t-1, k},\; 1 - (\phi^{(\sigma)})^2\right), \label{eq:ar1-sigma}
\end{align}
with $|\phi^{(w)}|, |\phi^{(z)}|, |\phi^{(\sigma)}| < 1$, and initial values $\bw^*_{1k} \sim N_2(\bZero, \bI_2)$, $z^*_{1k} \sim N(0, 1)$, $\sigma^{2*}_{1k} \sim N(0, 1)$.  Transforming back through \eref{eq:wstar}--\eref{eq:sigmastar} gives $\bw_{tk} \sim \mathrm{Unif}(\calD)$, $z_{tk} \sim \mathrm{HN}(0, 1)$, and $\sigma^2_{tk} \sim \mathrm{IG}(a/2, b/2)$ at every $t$, so that the spatial \skewt structure of \eref{eq:fullmodel} is preserved at each day.

\subsection{Extremal dependence}\label{sec:extdep}

For exceedance prediction, what matters is how strongly the \skewt process couples extreme values at two sites, and how that coupling changes with their separation.
One measure of this extremal dependence is the $\chi$ statistic \parencite{Coles1999}, which for a stationary and isotropic spatial process at separation $h$ is
\begin{align} \label{eq:chih}
  \chi(h) = \lim_{c \rightarrow c^*} \Pr[Y(\bs + h) > c \mid Y(\bs) > c],
\end{align}
where $c^*$ is the upper limit of the support of $Y$; for the \skewt distribution $c^* = \infty$.
If $\chi(h) = 0$, then observations are asymptotically independent at distance $h$.
For Gaussian processes, $\chi(h) = 0$ regardless of the distance $h$, so they are not suitable for modeling asymptotically dependent extremes.
Unlike the Gaussian process, the \skewt process is asymptotically dependent.
However, one problem with the spatial \skewt process is that $\lim_{h \rightarrow \infty} \chi(h) > 0$.
This occurs because all observations, both near and far, share the same $z$ and $\sigma$ terms.
Such long-range dependence is undesirable over large domains, where only local dependence is expected.
\textcite{morris_space-time_2017} avoid this problem through a random partitioning mechanism, described in detail in \sref{sec:sttskewt}.

\subsection{Extension to AR(2) latent processes}\label{sec:ar2}

In this section, we generalize the AR(1) prior \eref{eq:ar1-w}--\eref{eq:ar1-sigma} on the transformed latents to a stationary AR(2) prior.

\paragraph{The AR(2) prior.}
Writing $\{Y_t\}$ for any scalar component of $\bw^*_{tk}$, or for $z^*_{tk}$ or $\sigma^{2*}_{tk}$ at a fixed knot $k$, the prior is the stationary AR(2) recursion
\begin{align}
  Y_t \;=\; \phi_1\, Y_{t-1} \;+\; \phi_2\, Y_{t-2} \;+\; \epsilon_t,
  \qquad \epsilon_t \iid N(0, \sigma_\epsilon^2),
  \qquad t \ge 3, \label{eq:ar2-recursion}
\end{align}
with $(\phi_1, \phi_2)$ confined to the stationarity triangle
\begin{align}
  \calS \;=\; \big\{ (\phi_1, \phi_2) \;:\; |\phi_2| < 1, \;\; \phi_1 + \phi_2 < 1, \;\; \phi_2 - \phi_1 < 1 \big\}. \label{eq:stab-triangle}
\end{align}
Because the inverse PIT \eref{eq:wstar}--\eref{eq:sigmastar} returns the half-normal, inverse-gamma, and uniform target margins only when $\gamma_0 := \mathrm{Var}(Y_t) = 1$, the innovation variance is not free.  Solving the Yule--Walker equations under $\gamma_0 = 1$ (Appendix~\ref{app:ar2-moments}) fixes the standardized autocovariances
\begin{align}
  \gamma_1 \;=\; \frac{\phi_1}{1 - \phi_2}, \qquad
  \gamma_2 \;=\; \phi_1\, \gamma_1 + \phi_2, \label{eq:gamma12}
\end{align}
\begin{align}
  \sigma_\epsilon^2 \;=\; 1 - \phi_1\, \gamma_1 - \phi_2\, \gamma_2. \label{eq:sigeps}
\end{align}
Setting $\phi_2 = 0$ gives $\gamma_1 = \phi_1$ and $\sigma_\epsilon^2 = 1 - \phi_1^2$, the AR(1) form of \eref{eq:ar1-w}--\eref{eq:ar1-sigma}, so the AR(2) prior strictly nests the prior of \textcite{morris_space-time_2017}.

\paragraph{Stationary initial pair.}
The recursion \eref{eq:ar2-recursion} needs two seeds, and independent $N(0, 1)$ seeds would leave $\mathrm{Var}(Y_t) \ne 1$ over the first days and break the margin.  We therefore draw the initial pair from the stationary bivariate Gaussian
\begin{align}
  \begin{pmatrix} Y_1 \\ Y_2 \end{pmatrix}
  \;\sim\;
  N_2\!\left(
    \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix},\;
    \begin{pmatrix} 1 & \gamma_1 \\ \gamma_1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}
  \right), \label{eq:joint-init}
\end{align}
so that $\mathrm{Var}(Y_t) = 1$ holds from $t = 1$ onward.

\paragraph{Application to the three transformed parameters.}
Mirroring the AR(1) block \eref{eq:ar1-w}--\eref{eq:ar1-sigma}, the AR(2) prior \eref{eq:ar2-recursion} with initial condition \eref{eq:joint-init} replaces it component-wise:
\begin{align}
  \bw^*_{tk} \mid \bw^*_{t-1, k}, \bw^*_{t-2, k}
    &\sim N_2\!\left(\phi^{(w)}_1\, \bw^*_{t-1, k} + \phi^{(w)}_2\, \bw^*_{t-2, k},\; \sigma_{\epsilon, w}^2\, \bI_2\right), \label{eq:ar2-w}\\
  z^*_{tk} \mid z^*_{t-1, k}, z^*_{t-2, k}
    &\sim N\!\left(\phi^{(z)}_1\, z^*_{t-1, k} + \phi^{(z)}_2\, z^*_{t-2, k},\; \sigma_{\epsilon, z}^2\right), \label{eq:ar2-z}\\
  \sigma^{2*}_{tk} \mid \sigma^{2*}_{t-1, k}, \sigma^{2*}_{t-2, k}
    &\sim N\!\left(\phi^{(\sigma)}_1\, \sigma^{2*}_{t-1, k} + \phi^{(\sigma)}_2\, \sigma^{2*}_{t-2, k},\; \sigma_{\epsilon, \sigma}^2\right), \label{eq:ar2-sigma}
\end{align}
where each pair $\bphi^{(\cdot)}$ lies in the stationarity triangle $\calS$ of \eref{eq:stab-triangle}, the innovation variances follow from \eref{eq:gamma12}--\eref{eq:sigeps}, and the initial pairs follow \eref{eq:joint-init} component-wise.  After the inverse PIT \eref{eq:wstar}--\eref{eq:sigmastar}, the back-transformed $\bw_{tk}, z_{tk}, \sigma^2_{tk}$ keep the same marginals as the AR(1) baseline, so the spatial \skewt model \eref{eq:fullmodel} is preserved at every $t$.  The Gibbs-within-MH full conditional that updates each $Y_t$ now carries three prior factors (self, lag-1, lag-2); its explicit form and the Metropolis--Hastings acceptance ratio are given in Appendix~\ref{app:full-cond}.

\subsection{Incorporating MRTS covariates}\label{sec:meth-mrts}

\textcite{tzeng_resolution_2018} proposed the multi-resolution thin-plate spline (MRTS) basis, whose functions are ordered from coarse to increasingly fine resolution. This ordering lets a model add spatial detail one resolution at a time; we define the basis below on a $2$-dimensional spatial domain. Consider $n$ distinct locations, $\bs_1, \cdots, \bs_n$, where $\bs_i = (x_{i1}, x_{i2})^\top \in \calR^2$ for $i = 1, \ldots, n$, and let
\begin{equation}
  \bR \;=\;
  \begin{pmatrix}
    1 & x_{11} & x_{12} \\
    \vdots & \vdots & \vdots \\
    1 & x_{n1} & x_{n2}
  \end{pmatrix}.
  \label{eq:mrts-X}
\end{equation}
Then the MRTS basis functions $f_k(\bs)$ at a location $\bs = (x_1, x_2)^\top$ are defined as
\begin{equation}
  f_k(\bs) \;=\;
  \begin{cases}
    1, & k = 1, \\
    x_{k-1}, & k = 2, 3, \\
    \mu_{k-3}^{-1} \big\{ \bpsi(\bs) \,-\, \bPsi \bR( \bR^\top \bR )^{-1} \, \br \big\}^\top \bv_{k-3}, & k = 4, \ldots, n,
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:mrts-basis}
\end{equation}
where $\br = (1, x_1, x_2)^\top$, $\bpsi(\bs) = (\psi_1(\bs), \ldots, \psi_n(\bs))^\top$ with
\begin{equation}
  \psi_i(\bs) \;=\; \frac{1}{8\pi}\,\|\bs - \bs_i\|^2 \, \log\!\big(\|\bs - \bs_i\|\big), \qquad i = 1, \ldots, n,
  \label{eq:tps-kernel}
\end{equation}
and $\bPsi$ is the $n \times n$ matrix with $(i,j)$-th element $\psi_j(\bs_i)$, $\bv_k$ is the $k$-th column of $\bV$, and $\bV\,\mathrm{diag}(\mu_1, \ldots, \mu_n)\,\bV^\top$ is the eigen-decomposition of $\bQ\,\bPsi\,\bQ$ with $\mu_1 \ge \cdots \ge \mu_n$ and $\bQ = \bI - \bR(\bR^\top \bR)^{-1} \bR^\top$.

The MRTS basis enters the STP through the regression component. Fix the number of basis functions $K_{\mathrm{MRTS}} \ge 1$ and let
\begin{align}
 \bff(\bs) \;=\; \big(f_1(\bs),\, f_2(\bs),\, \ldots,\, f_{K_{\mathrm{MRTS}}}(\bs)\big)^\top
 \label{eq:mrts-f}
\end{align}
collect the first $K_{\mathrm{MRTS}}$ basis functions defined in \eref{eq:mrts-basis}. Then the day-$t$ covariate vector $\bX_t(\bs)$ in \eref{eq:fullmodel} is replaced by
\begin{align}
 \bX^*_t(\bs)
 &= \big[\bff(\bs)^\top,\; \bX_t(\bs)^\top\big]^\top \notag\\
 &= \big[f_1(\bs),\; f_2(\bs),\, \ldots,\, f_{K_{\mathrm{MRTS}}}(\bs),\; \mathrm{X}_1(\bs, t),\, \ldots,\, \mathrm{X}_p(\bs, t)\big]^\top,
 \label{eq:mrts-augmented}
\end{align}
where $\mathrm{X}_j(\bs, t)$ represents the $j$-th exogenous covariate of $\bX_t(\bs)$. The model in \eref{eq:fullmodel} can then be expressed as
\begin{align}
  Y_t(\bs) \;=\; \bX^*_t(\bs)^\top \bbeta^* \;+\; \lambda\, \sigma_t(\bs)\, |z_t(\bs)| \;+\; \sigma_t(\bs)\, v_t(\bs),
  \label{eq:fullmodel-mrts}
\end{align}
which is identical to the STP model \eref{eq:fullmodel} on all components other than the mean, with $\bbeta^* \in \calR^{K_{\mathrm{MRTS}} + p}$.  Because the MRTS basis functions depend only on location, each site $\bs$ shares the same ordered set of basis values $\bff(\bs)$ at every day $t$.  The number of basis functions $K_{\mathrm{MRTS}}$ is the practical tuning parameter.  \sref{sec:sim-mrts-design} compares $K_{\mathrm{MRTS}} \in \{0, 3, 5, 8, 10, 12, 15, 20, 25\}$ across its simulation designs, extended by $\{30, 40, 50\}$ on the two nonlinear-mean designs.

\subsection{Hierarchical model}\label{sec:meth-hier}

We specify the model as a three-stage Bayesian hierarchical model in the sense
of \textcite{berliner_hierarchical_1996}, following the presentation of hierarchical spatial models
in \textcite{gelfand_handbook_2010}: a data stage conditional on the latent
process, a process stage conditional on the parameters, and a parameter stage
carrying the priors.  Collect the day-$t$ latent processes as
$\calZ_t = \{(\bw_{tk},\, z_{tk},\, \sigma^2_{tk}) : k = 1, \ldots, K\}$ and the
parameters as $\btheta = \{\bbeta, \lambda, \varphi, \nu, \eta, a, b, \bphi\}$,
where $\bphi$ collects the AR coefficients of the three latent processes.  The
number of knots $K$ is not treated as random; it indexes candidate models
selected by held-out Brier score (\sref{sec:sim-scoring}).

\paragraph{Stage 1: data model.}
The observations are the censored responses,
\begin{align}
  \widetilde{Y}_t(\bs_i) \;=\; \max\{Y_t(\bs_i),\, T\},
  \qquad i = 1, \ldots, n,\quad t = 1, \ldots, n_t,
  \label{eq:stage1-censor}
\end{align}
so that values above $T$ are observed exactly, while values below $T$ are known
only to lie below $T$ and are imputed within the MCMC.

\paragraph{Stage 2: process model.}
The first substage is the conditional Gaussian field: independently across days,
\begin{align}
  \bY_t \mid \calZ_t,\, \btheta
  \;\sim\;
  N_n\!\Big(\bX_t\bbeta \,+\, \lambda\, \bD_t\, |\bz_t|,\;\;
            \bD_t\, \bR(\varphi, \nu, \eta)\, \bD_t\Big),
  \label{eq:bhm-data-gauss}
\end{align}
where $\bD_t = \mathrm{diag}\{\sigma_t(\bs_1), \ldots, \sigma_t(\bs_n)\}$,
$|\bz_t| = (|z_t(\bs_1)|, \ldots, |z_t(\bs_n)|)^\top$, and
$\bR(\varphi, \nu, \eta)$ has entries $\rho(\|\bs_i - \bs_j\|)$ from
\eref{eq:matern}.  Under the MRTS extension, $\bX_t\bbeta$ is replaced by
$\bX^*_t\bbeta^*$ of \eref{eq:mrts-augmented}.

The second substage maps the subregion latents to the sites through the
partition: for $\bs \in P_{tk}$,
\begin{align}
  z_t(\bs) \;=\; z_{tk},
  \qquad
  \sigma^2_t(\bs) \;=\; \sigma^2_{tk},
  \qquad
  P_{tk} \;=\; \{\bs \in \calD : k = \argmin_\ell \|\bs - \bw_{t\ell}\|\}.
  \label{eq:stage2-partition}
\end{align}

The third substage gives the latent temporal dynamics on the transformed scale
of \eref{eq:wstar}--\eref{eq:sigmastar}, independently across knots
$k = 1, \ldots, K$: for $t \ge 3$,
\begin{align}
  \bw^*_{tk} \mid \bw^*_{t-1,k},\, \bw^*_{t-2,k},\, \bphi^{(w)}
    &\sim N_2\!\big(\phi^{(w)}_1 \bw^*_{t-1,k} + \phi^{(w)}_2 \bw^*_{t-2,k},\;
                    \sigma^2_{\epsilon,w}\, \bI_2\big),
    \label{eq:stage2-w}\\
  z^*_{tk} \mid z^*_{t-1,k},\, z^*_{t-2,k},\, \bphi^{(z)}
    &\sim N\!\big(\phi^{(z)}_1 z^*_{t-1,k} + \phi^{(z)}_2 z^*_{t-2,k},\;
                  \sigma^2_{\epsilon,z}\big),
    \label{eq:stage2-z}\\
  \sigma^{2*}_{tk} \mid \sigma^{2*}_{t-1,k},\, \sigma^{2*}_{t-2,k},\, \bphi^{(\sigma)}
    &\sim N\!\big(\phi^{(\sigma)}_1 \sigma^{2*}_{t-1,k}
                  + \phi^{(\sigma)}_2 \sigma^{2*}_{t-2,k},\;
                  \sigma^2_{\epsilon,\sigma}\big),
    \label{eq:stage2-sigma}
\end{align}
with each innovation variance fixed by the Yule--Walker standardization
\eref{eq:gamma12}--\eref{eq:sigeps} and each initial pair drawn from the
stationary bivariate Gaussian \eref{eq:joint-init}.  The AR(1) baseline of
\textcite{morris_space-time_2017} is the special case $\phi^{(\cdot)}_2 = 0$,
which reduces \eref{eq:stage2-w}--\eref{eq:stage2-sigma} to
\eref{eq:ar1-w}--\eref{eq:ar1-sigma}.  The inverse transforms
\eref{eq:wstar}--\eref{eq:sigmastar} return the marginals
$\bw_{tk} \sim \mathrm{Unif}(\calD)$, $z_{tk} \sim \mathrm{HN}(0,1)$, and
$\sigma^2_{tk} \sim \mathrm{IG}(a/2,\, b/2)$ at every $t$.

\paragraph{Stage 3: parameter model.}
All parameters, and only parameters, receive priors at this stage:
\begin{align}
  \bbeta &\sim N_p(\bZero,\, \sigma^2_\beta \bI),
  &
  \lambda &\sim N(0,\, \sigma^2_\lambda),
  &
  \eta &\sim \mathrm{Unif}(0, 1),
  \nonumber\\
  \varphi &\sim \mathrm{Unif}(0,\, \varphi_{\max}),
  &
  a/2 &\sim \mathrm{Unif}\{0.05,\, 0.10,\, \ldots,\, 5\},
  &
  b/2 &\sim \mathrm{Gamma}(1,\, 1),
  \nonumber\\
  \log \nu &\sim N(-1.2,\, 1),\ \nu \le 10,
  &
  \bphi^{(\cdot)}
    &\sim N_2\big(\bZero,\, \sigma^2_\phi \bI_2\big)\,
      I\big(\bphi^{(\cdot)} \in \calS\big).
  \label{eq:stage3-priors}
\end{align}
Here $\mathrm{Unif}\{\cdot\}$ denotes the discrete uniform distribution on the
listed grid, and the hyperparameters are $\sigma_\beta = \sigma_\lambda = 20$
and $\sigma_\phi = 0.5$,
with the range bound $\varphi_{\max} = 15$ in the simulation study and
$\varphi_{\max} = 5$ in the ozone study, matched to the extent of each spatial
domain.  The smoothness $\nu$ receives a lognormal prior with median
$e^{-1.2} \approx 0.30$, truncated above at $10$.  Both autoregressive
coefficients carry the
prior scale $\sigma_\phi$ of the AR(1) baseline, so the AR(1) and AR(2) fits
are matched on prior scale rather than tuned separately.

Writing $[\,\cdot\,]$ for a generic distribution, the three stages multiply to
the joint model, and by Bayes' Rule the posterior is proportional to that
product,
\begin{align}
  \big[\bY_{\mathrm{cens}},\, \calZ,\, \btheta \mid \widetilde{\bY}\big]
  \;\propto\;
  \prod_{t=1}^{n_t}
  \big[\widetilde{\bY}_t \mid \bY_t\big]\,
  \big[\bY_t \mid \calZ_t,\, \btheta\big]\;
  \big[\calZ_{1:n_t} \mid \btheta\big]\,
  \big[\btheta\big],
  \label{eq:bhm-posterior}
\end{align}
sampled by Gibbs-within-MH updates: each factor of
\eref{eq:bhm-posterior} is one of the stages above, and the full conditionals
are read off stage by stage; the full conditionals of the time-varying
parameters and of the autoregressive coefficients, together with their
Metropolis--Hastings acceptance ratios, are given in
Appendix~\ref{app:full-cond}.  Predictions at
unmonitored sites follow from the conditional Gaussian structure of
\eref{eq:bhm-data-gauss} (Bayesian kriging).

\section{Simulation study}\label{sec:simulation}

This simulation study examines whether, and under which data-generating settings, the AR(2) and MRTS extensions improve held-out exceedance prediction.  \sref{sec:sim-design} gives the design shared by all experiments and \sref{sec:sim-scoring} the validation protocols, including the uncertainty attached to every score comparison.  \sref{sec:sim-ar2-design} evaluates the AR(2) extension under autoregressive, highly persistent, and long-memory temporal dynamics; \sref{sec:sim-mrts-design} evaluates the MRTS extension under second-moment and first-moment spatial non-stationarity.  Throughout, both extensions are assessed as candidate options, benchmarked against Gaussian fits and against matched models without each extension.

\subsection{Common simulation design}\label{sec:sim-design}

All simulation designs generate data from the hierarchical model of \sref{sec:meth-hier} on $n_s = 144$ sites, drawn uniformly on $[0,10] \times [0,10]$, over $n_t = 50$ days ($n_t = 200$ in the time-block forecast evaluation of \sref{sec:sim-ar2-design}); the single exception is the pair of nonlinear-mean designs of \sref{sec:sim-mrts-design}, which replace the \skewt error process with a Gaussian one.  The \Matern correlation \eref{eq:matern} is generated at $\eta = 0.9$, $\nu = 1/2$, and $\varphi = 1$ in every design.  In the data generation the regression mean is held constant, $\bX^\top \bbeta = 10$ (\sref{sec:sim-mrts-design} adds a mean-zero non-stationary random effect to one design, which leaves the regression mean at $10$, and a fixed nonlinear mean surface to its two Gaussian-error designs), while every fitted model includes an intercept and a first-order spatial trend.  For each design we simulate $10$ data sets, each split into $100$ training sites and $44$ withheld test sites, and run every MCMC chain for $20{,}000$ iterations with a burn-in period of $10{,}000$ iterations.  Throughout, $q(0.80)$ denotes the $80$th sample quantile of the data, and the five baseline analysis models are
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=3em]
  \item Gaussian marginal, $K = 1$ knot;
  \item \Skewt marginal, $K = 1$ knot, no thresholding;
  \item Symmetric-$t$ marginal, $K = 1$ knot, threshold $T = q(0.80)$;
  \item \Skewt marginal, $K = 5$ knots, no thresholding;
  \item Symmetric-$t$ marginal, $K = 5$ knots, threshold $T = q(0.80)$.
\end{enumerate}

Censoring is paired with the symmetric-$t$ marginal because the skewness parameter is only weakly identified once the lower tail is censored, following \textcite{morris_space-time_2017}.

\subsection{Validation protocols and uncertainty}\label{sec:sim-scoring}

\paragraph{Spatial hold-out validation.}
Models are compared on held-out sites, with $100$ training sites fitting each setting and $44$ withheld sites scoring its predictions on each replicate.  Because the target is exceedance of a high level, we compare models by the Brier score \parencite{gneiting_strictly_2007}.  For an exceedance level $L$, the Brier score is
\begin{align}
  \mathrm{BS}(L) \;=\; \big\{\, I(y > L) \;-\; \widehat{P}(Y > L) \,\big\}^2,
  \label{eq:brier-score}
\end{align}
where $y$ is the held-out observation, $I(y > L)$ is the exceedance indicator, and $\widehat{P}(Y > L)$ is the posterior predictive probability of exceeding $L$.  We average the Brier scores over all test sites and days; lower values indicate better exceedance prediction, and $L$ ranges over the sample quantiles reported with each figure.

\paragraph{Time-block forecast validation.}\label{sec:sim-timeblock-cv}

The site-splitting scheme above scores interpolation.  When a withheld site is predicted, every day of the record is observed at the training sites.  The time-block forecast evaluation of \sref{sec:sim-ar2-design} scores temporal extrapolation instead, with all $144$ sites observed for days $1$--$50$ and predictions evaluated at the same sites on the following $15$ days.  For each forecast lead $h$ the Brier score \eref{eq:brier-score} is computed with $L$ set to the empirical $0.95$ quantile of the validation block (the same threshold for every model), averaged over sites, and then over data sets; models are compared pairwise within each data set.  Short-record fits whose skewness parameter and intercept fail to separate, or whose predictive spread is unbounded, are screened before scoring.

\paragraph{Uncertainty of score comparisons.}\label{sec:sim-uq}

A mean Brier score is an estimate, and two such estimates computed on the same held-out sites are strongly positively correlated, since a site that is hard to predict is hard for every model.  We therefore attach uncertainty to the \emph{paired difference} of scores.  For a matched pair of models $A$ and $B$ and an exceedance level $L$, the estimand is
\begin{align}
  \Delta(L) \;=\; \mathrm{E}\big[\mathrm{BS}_A(L)\big] \;-\; \mathrm{E}\big[\mathrm{BS}_B(L)\big],
  \label{eq:delta-estimand}
\end{align}
estimated by forming the difference site by site on the shared held-out set, so that the common hard-site variation cancels, and assessing its sampling variability over the $10$ simulated data sets.  Each comparison tests $H_0\!: \Delta(L) = 0$ against the two-sided alternative, summarized by a $95\%$ paired-$t$ confidence interval and a Wilcoxon signed-rank test, which does not assume normal differences; with $10$ replicates the Wilcoxon $p$-values move on a coarse grid whose smallest attainable two-sided value is $2^{-9} \approx 0.002$.  Each pair varies only the factor under test, with the analysis knot count matched to the generating design.  In the tables that follow, a star marks an interval that excludes zero; among the dozens of paired comparisons reported in this thesis, an isolated borderline star is within what chance alone would produce, and an interval that covers zero means the data fail to reject $H_0$, not that the two models have been shown equal.  In the ozone study of \sref{sec:ozonedata} there is a single data set and hence no replicate layer, so the resampling unit is instead the validation site, as described there.

\subsection{The AR(2) experiment}\label{sec:sim-ar2-design}

\paragraph{Design.}
To test the AR(2) analysis model against data with a known autoregressive structure, the six generating designs are \skewt ($\lambda = 3$) with temporal dependence imposed on the latent $\sigma^2$, $\bw$, and $z$ processes.  The first five evolve through a fixed AR(2) recursion \eref{eq:ar2-w}--\eref{eq:ar2-sigma}; the sixth replaces it with a long-memory ARFIMA$(0, d, 0)$ process:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=3em]
  \item $K = 1$ knot, strong dependence $(\phi_1, \phi_2) = (0.80, -0.35)$;
  \item $K = 1$ knot, weak dependence $(\phi_1, \phi_2) = (0.12, -0.05)$;
  \item $K = 5$ knots, strong dependence $(\phi_1, \phi_2) = (0.80, -0.35)$;
  \item $K = 5$ knots, weak dependence $(\phi_1, \phi_2) = (0.12, -0.05)$;
  \item $K = 1$ knot, persistent AR(2) $(\phi_1, \phi_2) = (0.15, 0.80)$;
  \item $K = 1$ knot, long-memory ARFIMA$(0, d, 0)$, $d = 0.45$ \parencite{davies_tests_1987}.
\end{enumerate}
All three AR(2) coefficient pairs lie inside the stationarity triangle $\calS$.  Every design is fit by the same nine analysis models, namely the five baselines and the \skewt models with AR(1) and AR(2) coefficients at $K = 1$ and $K = 5$ knots.

The persistent and long-memory designs (5 and 6) are evaluated under both protocols of \sref{sec:sim-scoring}.  The spatial hold-out evaluation fits the same nine models as the other designs; the time-block forecast evaluation fits the $K = 1$ \skewt variants with the latent processes i.i.d.\ in time, with AR(1) structure, and with AR(2) structure.  As a short-memory control, the fixed-AR(2) design with $(\phi_1, \phi_2) = (0.80, -0.35)$, whose autocorrelation decays within a few days, is run under the same forecast protocol with the i.i.d.\ and AR(2) variants.

\subsection{The MRTS experiment}\label{sec:sim-mrts-design}

\paragraph{Design.}
This experiment enriches the regression component with the MRTS basis functions $f_1(\bs), \ldots, f_{K_{\mathrm{MRTS}}}(\bs)$ of \sref{sec:meth-mrts}.  The four generating designs place the non-stationarity first where the mean basis cannot reach it (designs 1--2) and then where it can (designs 3--4):
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=3em]
  \item \Skewt, $K = 5$ knots, $\lambda = 3$, with the constant mean $\bX^\top \bbeta = 10$;
  \item \Skewt, $K = 1$ knot, $\lambda = 3$, with an additive mean-zero non-stationary spatial random effect $u_t(\bs)$, redrawn daily from two fixed cosine fields (Appendix~\ref{app:nsdep-cov}), so the non-stationarity lies in the second moment while the marginal mean stays at $10$;
  \item Gaussian, sigmoid front: the nonlinear-mean model \eref{eq:nont-gen} with $\tilde g(\bs) = \tanh\{\lVert \bs - (2, 2)^\top \rVert - 4.5\}$, a smoothed circular step;
  \item Gaussian, transform mix: the nonlinear-mean model \eref{eq:nont-gen} with $\tilde g(\bs) = \mathrm{std}\{\log(1 + s_1)\} + \mathrm{std}\{e^{-s_1/2}\} + \mathrm{std}\{s_1 / (1 + s_2)\}$, with $\mathrm{std}\{\cdot\}$ standardizing each term to unit spatial standard deviation, whose curvature concentrates near the domain boundary, where the uniform site draw is sparsest.
\end{enumerate}

The nonlinear-mean designs replace the \skewt generator with Gaussian errors,
\begin{align}
  Y_t(\bs) \;=\; 10 \;+\; g(\bs) \;+\; \sigma_g\, \varepsilon_t(\bs), \qquad \sigma_g = 4,
  \label{eq:nont-gen}
\end{align}
where the errors $\varepsilon_t$ are standard Gaussian processes with the \Matern correlation \eref{eq:matern} at the generating values of \sref{sec:sim-design}, drawn independently across days; every $t$-family analysis model is therefore misspecified in its error law.  Each raw surface $\tilde g$ is orthogonalized against the baseline design $[1, s_1, s_2]$ and standardized to a spatial standard deviation of $11$, so the $K_{\mathrm{MRTS}} = 0$ design spans none of the resulting $g$.

Each of the five baseline models is fit across a grid of MRTS basis sizes, $K_{\mathrm{MRTS}} \in \{0, 3, 5, 8, 10, 12, 15, 20, 25\}$, where $K_{\mathrm{MRTS}} = 0$ recovers the no-MRTS baseline; on both nonlinear-mean designs the grid is extended by $\{30, 40, 50\}$, since the finer surfaces are resolved only by a fine basis.  All four designs are run with $10$ data sets.

\paragraph{Results.}\label{sec:sim-mrts-results}
On the two \skewt-core designs, augmenting the regression component with MRTS basis functions does not deliver a stable improvement in upper-tail scores, and enlarging the basis does not lower the average Brier score on either setting (\fref{fig:bs-mean-vs-K-mrts}).  On the five-knot setting every curve is essentially flat in $K_{\mathrm{MRTS}}$; on the additive non-stationary random-effect process the curves are likewise flat, with over-specified models destabilizing at large $K_{\mathrm{MRTS}}$.  As in the AR(2) experiment, the generating knot count dominates the ranking.  The second setting is the decisive one.  The non-stationarity lives in the second moment (a daily-redrawn random effect $u_t$), whereas the MRTS basis enters only the fixed-effect mean.  Because $u_t$ has zero time-average, the time-pooled coefficient $\bbeta^*$ has no first-moment structure to recover, so the Brier- and recovery-versus-$K$ curves stay flat by construction rather than through absence of signal, since $u_t$ contributes substantial (spatially non-stationary) variance.  The MRTS mean term therefore yields no reliable tail-score gain.  A fixed-effect mean cannot represent a non-stationary second-moment feature, and so, in these designs, the spatial mean is not the binding constraint on upper-tail prediction.

The two nonlinear-mean designs address the complementary case (\fref{fig:bs-mean-vs-K-nont}; Table~\ref{tab:mrts-nont-brier}).  On the sigmoid-front design the Brier score finally responds to the mean basis, but only once the basis is fine enough to resolve the front.  The profiles stay flat for a coarse basis, deteriorate slightly at intermediate sizes, and improve only at the finer bases, with the gains larger at the higher exceedance level.  The intermediate-basis deterioration is the signature of a too-coarse basis.  A partially fitted mean sharpens the predictive distribution while leaving a residual surface error that extrapolates to the held-out sites, and the net effect on the Brier score is negative until the basis actually resolves the front.

The transform-mix design shows the same mechanism operating from a much worse starting point.  The mismatched five-knot partition, compounded for the thresholded variant by censoring, leaves the no-basis baselines of the five-knot models far above those of the one-knot models, and the mean basis recovers most of that loss.  Every model improves at the $0.95$ threshold quantile, and at its best basis size each converges to nearly the same score.  The improvement is not uniform across levels, however.  At the rarer $0.98$ quantile it is carried entirely by the five-knot models, whereas the one-knot models stay flat or worsen.  This is the sharpness--extrapolation signature again, in that only the models whose no-basis fit is badly mis-specified, here the five-knot partition, carry enough headroom for the mean basis to convert into a gain at the rarest level.  Both surfaces were chosen, by an oracle calculation performed before any fitting, to leave genuine Brier headroom.  Replacing the fitted mean by the true surface $g$ under the true Gaussian error law leaves substantial headroom on both.  The realized gains convert only a small part of that headroom, which is consistent with the sharpness--extrapolation account above.  The Brier score improves only where the residual basis error is small relative to the predictive spread.

\subsection{Summary of the simulation study}\label{sec:sim-summary}

Overall, the simulation study indicates that both extensions enlarge the usable model class but that neither uniformly dominates the STP baseline.  The persistence experiment sharpens this conclusion into a statement about the prediction task itself.  Even under highly persistent and long-memory dependence, with the temporal coefficients well identified, spatial hold-out prediction is insensitive to the autoregressive order; modest gains from richer temporal pooling, directionally consistent though never statistically significant, emerge only once the task requires extrapolating beyond the observed record.  Whatever promise the AR(2) extension retains therefore lies in forecasting rather than interpolation.  The MRTS experiments admit an equally structural summary.  The mean term is inert when the non-stationarity lives in the second moment, and under nonlinear mean structure it pays off only once the basis is fine enough to resolve the surface, converting a small fraction of the oracle headroom.  Whether the same pattern holds on real environmental data is the question taken up in \sref{sec:ozonedata}, where the AR(2) and MRTS variants enter as candidate options to be selected by held-out predictive scores rather than as default replacements for the AR(1) STP baseline.

\section{Ozone data analysis}\label{sec:ozonedata}

\textcite{morris_space-time_2017} introduced the spatio-temporal \skewt (STP) process to predict high-threshold ground-level ozone exceedances from U.S. EPA monitoring data.  We return to the same July 2005 data, asking not whether a new model class is needed but whether enlarging the STP candidate class along the two axes of this thesis, the AR(2) temporal prior and the MRTS spatial-mean term, yields better held-out tail prediction.  The comparison is therefore designed to identify the exceedance levels, if any, at which the enlarged candidate class improves on the STP baseline, rather than to select a single overall-best model.

\subsection{Data}\label{sec:ozone-data-desc}

The response is the daily maximum 8-hour ozone concentration for the $31$ days of July 2005 at U.S. EPA Air Quality System (AQS) monitoring sites, with the co-located Community Multi-scale Air Quality (CMAQ) model output as the sole covariate.  \fref{fig:ozone-maps} summarizes the two data sources through the EPA design value, the fourth-highest daily maximum 8-hour average of the month.  The AQS monitors are irregularly spaced, covering the eastern United States and California densely but leaving large gaps in the interior west, whereas CMAQ provides complete gridded coverage of the conterminous United States.  CMAQ reproduces the broad spatial pattern of the observations but not their level at individual monitors, which motivates using it as a regression covariate to be calibrated rather than as a substitute for the observations.  Following \textcite{morris_space-time_2017}, the regression component is $\bX_t(\bs) = [1,\text{CMAQ}_t(\bs)]^\top$, so that each model acts as a statistical calibration of CMAQ against the monitor observations.

\subsection{Candidate models and validation design}\label{sec:ozone-models}

The baseline candidate set follows that of \textcite{morris_space-time_2017}.  We fit Gaussian and \skewt marginals over a range of partition sizes, $K \in \{1,5,6,7,8,9,10,15\}$, and censor the response at three levels to contrast no, moderate, and high censoring, namely no censoring ($T=0$), a moderate threshold ($T=50$ ppb, the $0.42$ sample quantile), and a high threshold ($T=75$ ppb, the $0.92$ sample quantile).  For settings censored at $T=75$ ppb, we use a symmetric-$t$ marginal, as in the simulation study.  Each setting is fit both with and without a latent time series.  

On top of this baseline we add the two extensions developed in this thesis.  The first replaces the AR(1) latent dynamics with the standardized AR(2) prior of \sref{sec:ar2}; the second augments the regression component with MRTS basis functions, so that non-stationary spatial mean structure can be absorbed by multi-resolution thin-plate splines.  Unlike the simulation study, which sweeps a grid of basis sizes, the ozone candidate set fixes $K_{\mathrm{MRTS}} = 10$, so the MRTS comparison below is a pre-specified one rather than a best-over-$K_{\mathrm{MRTS}}$ selection.

Of the $1{,}089$ sites of \fref{fig:ozone-maps}, the comparison draws on a stratified sample of $800$, drawn to represent each region of the United States equally so that the interior west is not swamped by the dense eastern network.  Models are evaluated by two-fold cross validation, with the $800$ sites split at random into $400$ training and $400$ validation sites per fold, scored by the held-out Brier score of \sref{sec:sim-scoring}; robustness of the ranking to the split is examined with two further splits in \sref{sec:ozone-results}.  Each MCMC chain is run for $30{,}000$ iterations with a burn-in period of $25{,}000$ iterations.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

This thesis asked not whether the spatio-temporal \skewt process predicts
high-threshold exceedances well, which \textcite{morris_space-time_2017} already
established, but where within it room for sharper upper-tail prediction
remains.  The temporal axis divides along the prediction task.  Added memory
leaves spatial interpolation unchanged, and not for want of signal: under
persistent and long-memory dynamics the coefficients were recovered closely and
the held-out scores still did not move, because with the full record observed
at the training sites the daily cross-sectional information dominates.  Only
forecasting beyond the observed record rewarded richer temporal pooling, and
then by margins never statistically significant.  The spatial axis is favorable
but small, the MRTS mean term is inert against second-moment non-stationarity,
and the real improvement it bought on the ozone data was one only the largest
validation set could detect.  Two caveats bound these conclusions, the evidence
covers a single month of a single pollutant, and the forecast evaluation used a
single origin.

The implication extends beyond ozone, since the same target, a spatial
probability statement about the upper tail at unmonitored sites, arises for any
heavy-tailed, asymmetric environmental field regulated by a high threshold.
Temporal order and mean flexibility are not free improvements to such a model,
but neither are they idle.  Rather than replacing the STP baseline, the
standardized AR(2) prior and the MRTS mean enlarge it into a candidate class
from which held-out tail scores can select the setting that fits the data at
hand.  Where each axis fell short marks where to go next: entering the MRTS
basis with random coefficients, as in fixed-rank kriging, would place the
flexibility where the simulation showed the structure to be, while a
reparameterized $z$, longer records, and a rolling forecast origin would test
whether the extrapolation gains are real.

\section{Stationary moments of the standardized AR(2)}\label{app:ar2-moments}

We derive the Yule--Walker moments \eref{eq:gamma12}--\eref{eq:sigeps} used to standardize the AR(2) prior \eref{eq:ar2-recursion} to unit marginal variance.  Let $\gamma_h = \mathrm{Cov}(Y_t, Y_{t-h})$ be the stationary autocovariances, constrained so that $\gamma_0 = \mathrm{Var}(Y_t) = 1$.  Multiplying the recursion \eref{eq:ar2-recursion} by $Y_{t-h}$ for $h \ge 1$ and taking expectations, and using that $\epsilon_t$ is independent of $Y_{t-h}$ for the causal stationary solution, yields the Yule--Walker recursion
\begin{align}
  \gamma_h \;=\; \phi_1\, \gamma_{h-1} \;+\; \phi_2\, \gamma_{h-2}, \qquad h \ge 1. \label{eq:yw}
\end{align}
Evaluating \eref{eq:yw} at $h = 1$ and using the symmetry $\gamma_{-1} = \gamma_1$ with $\gamma_0 = 1$ gives $\gamma_1 = \phi_1 + \phi_2\, \gamma_1$, hence $\gamma_1 = \phi_1 / (1 - \phi_2)$, and at $h = 2$ gives $\gamma_2 = \phi_1\, \gamma_1 + \phi_2$; together these are \eref{eq:gamma12}.  Multiplying \eref{eq:ar2-recursion} instead by $Y_t$ and taking expectations, with $\mathrm{E}[Y_t\, \epsilon_t] = \sigma_\epsilon^2$ because $Y_t$ carries the current innovation, gives $\gamma_0 = \phi_1\, \gamma_1 + \phi_2\, \gamma_2 + \sigma_\epsilon^2$, and solving for $\sigma_\epsilon^2$ under $\gamma_0 = 1$ recovers \eref{eq:sigeps}.  The same normalization fixes the initial pair, which has unit variances and lag-1 covariance $\gamma_1$ and is therefore the stationary bivariate Gaussian \eref{eq:joint-init}.

\section{AR(2) full conditionals for the Gibbs-within-MH update}\label{app:full-cond}

\paragraph{Updating the time-varying parameters.}
The AR(2) Markov structure factorizes the joint prior of $(Y_1, \ldots, Y_{n_t})$ as
\begin{align}
  p(Y_1, \ldots, Y_{n_t})
  \;=\; p(Y_1, Y_2) \prod_{s = 3}^{n_t} p(Y_s \mid Y_{s-1}, Y_{s-2}), \label{eq:joint-prior}
\end{align}
with $p(Y_1, Y_2)$ given by \eref{eq:joint-init} and each conditional factor for $s \ge 3$ following \eref{eq:ar2-recursion}.  For an interior time point $3 \le t \le n_t - 2$, the value $Y_t$ enters \eref{eq:joint-prior} through three factors, namely the self factor $p(Y_t \mid Y_{t-1}, Y_{t-2})$, the lag-1 factor $p(Y_{t+1} \mid Y_t, Y_{t-1})$, and the lag-2 factor $p(Y_{t+2} \mid Y_{t+1}, Y_t)$.  The full conditional in the Gibbs-within-MH update of $Y_t$ is therefore
\begin{align}
  \pi(Y_t \mid Y_{-t}, \bY)
  \;\propto\;
  L(\bY \mid Y_t) \,\cdot\,
  \underbrace{p(Y_t \mid Y_{t-1}, Y_{t-2})}_{\text{self}}
  \,\cdot\,
  \underbrace{p(Y_{t+1} \mid Y_t, Y_{t-1})}_{\text{lag-1}}
  \,\cdot\,
  \underbrace{p(Y_{t+2} \mid Y_{t+1}, Y_t)}_{\text{lag-2}}, \label{eq:full-cond}
\end{align}
where $L(\bY \mid Y_t)$ is the spatial likelihood contributed by $Y_t$ through the partition and Gaussian process structure of \eref{eq:fullmodel}.  The lag-2 factor is absent for $t = n_t - 1$ and both lag-1 and lag-2 factors are absent for $t = n_t$.  At the head of the series the initial-pair density \eref{eq:joint-init} plays the role of the self factor: $Y_1$ enters only the initial-pair factor $p(Y_1, Y_2)$ and the conditional $p(Y_3 \mid Y_2, Y_1)$, while $Y_2$ enters $p(Y_1, Y_2)$, $p(Y_3 \mid Y_2, Y_1)$, and $p(Y_4 \mid Y_3, Y_2)$; in each case the factors referencing a non-existent time index are simply dropped.  Under a symmetric Gaussian random-walk proposal $Y_t^{(c)} \sim N(Y_t, s^2)$, the Metropolis--Hastings acceptance ratio is
\begin{align}
  R \;=\; \min\!\left(1,\; \frac{\pi(Y_t^{(c)} \mid Y_{-t}, \bY)}{\pi(Y_t \mid Y_{-t}, \bY)}\right), \label{eq:mh-ratio}
\end{align}
in which all three prior factors must appear; omitting the lag-2 factor distorts the stationary distribution of the chain away from the AR(2) joint and produces a lag-2 autocorrelation that does not match the Yule--Walker value in \eref{eq:gamma12}.  The proposal scale is adapted during the first half of the burn-in period, by factors $0.8$ and $1.2$ whenever the running acceptance rate leaves $[0.25, 0.50]$, and is held fixed thereafter, so that the sampling phase runs a time-homogeneous kernel.  The move is componentwise for $z^*$ and $\sigma^{2*}$, while for the knot locations the $2K$ coordinates of a given day are proposed and accepted as a single block, which leaves \eref{eq:mh-ratio} unchanged in form because the block proposal is again a symmetric random walk.

\paragraph{Updating the autoregressive coefficients.}
Restoring the knot index, write $Y_{tk}$ for the transformed series of knot $k$ and $N(x;\, \mu,\, \sigma^2)$ for the normal density evaluated at $x$.  Conditional on those series, $\bphi^{(\cdot)}$ is independent of the data and of every other parameter, so its full conditional carries only the AR(2) prior and the coefficient prior of \eref{eq:stage3-priors},
\begin{align}
  \pi\big(\bphi^{(\cdot)} \mid Y\big)
  \;\propto\;
  \Bigg[ \prod_{k=1}^{K} p\big(Y_{1k}, \ldots, Y_{n_t k} \mid \bphi^{(\cdot)}\big) \Bigg]\,
  N_2\big(\bphi^{(\cdot)};\, \bZero,\, \sigma^2_\phi \bI_2\big)\,
  I\big(\bphi^{(\cdot)} \in \calS\big),
  \label{eq:phi-full-cond}
\end{align}
the product running over the $K$ knots, which are independent replicates of the same recursion, and over both coordinates in the case of $\bw^*$.  Expanding each factor by \eref{eq:joint-prior} and discarding $p(Y_{1k}) = N(0, 1)$, which does not involve $\bphi^{(\cdot)}$, the log-likelihood in \eref{eq:phi-full-cond} is
\begin{align}
  \ell\big(\bphi^{(\cdot)}\big)
  \;=\;
  \sum_{k=1}^{K} \log N\big(Y_{2k};\; \gamma_1 Y_{1k},\; 1 - \gamma_1^2\big)
  \;+\;
  \sum_{k=1}^{K} \sum_{t=3}^{n_t}
    \log N\big(Y_{tk};\; \phi_1 Y_{t-1,k} + \phi_2 Y_{t-2,k},\; \sigma_\epsilon^2\big),
  \label{eq:phi-loglik}
\end{align}
with $\gamma_1$ and $\sigma_\epsilon^2$ the functions of $\bphi^{(\cdot)}$ fixed by \eref{eq:gamma12}--\eref{eq:sigeps}.  The first sum is the contribution of the initial pair and cannot be dropped, because it depends on the coefficients through $\gamma_1 = \phi_1 / (1 - \phi_2)$.  Keeping only the transitions would leave the coefficient update targeting the conditional likelihood while the update of $Y_t$ above targets the full joint, so that the two blocks of the sampler no longer share a stationary distribution.  Under AR(1) the same omission is harmless, since there the only discarded factor is $p(Y_1) = N(0, 1)$ and the day-$2$ factor is itself a transition.

The two coefficients are drawn one at a time, the second conditioning on the value of the first just accepted.  For $\phi_1$ a candidate is proposed from the untruncated random walk $\phi_1^{(c)} \sim N(\phi_1, s_\phi^2)$ and, when $(\phi_1^{(c)}, \phi_2) \in \calS$, is accepted with probability
\begin{align}
  \min\!\left(1,\;
    \frac{\exp\{\ell(\phi_1^{(c)}, \phi_2)\}\; N\big(\phi_1^{(c)};\, 0,\, \sigma^2_\phi\big)}
         {\exp\{\ell(\phi_1, \phi_2)\}\; N\big(\phi_1;\, 0,\, \sigma^2_\phi\big)}\right),
  \label{eq:phi-mh-ratio}
\end{align}
while a candidate falling outside $\calS$ is rejected outright and the chain holds at its current value; $\phi_2$ is drawn the same way with the roles of the two coefficients exchanged, and $s_\phi$ is adapted by the rule stated above.  Rejecting outside $\calS$ needs no Hastings adjustment.  The proposal is symmetric and the acceptance region $\calS$ does not depend on the current state, so for any two points of $\calS$ the proposal density is the same in both directions, detailed balance holds with respect to \eref{eq:phi-full-cond} restricted to $\calS$, and the mass proposed outside $\calS$ only raises the probability of staying put.  A proposal truncated to $\calS$ together with the matching asymmetry correction, the scheme used by the AR(1) baseline, is equally valid but defines a different chain.  One pair $\bphi^{(\cdot)}$ is shared by all $K$ knots of its process.

\section{Marginal covariance of the non-stationary-dependence design}\label{app:nsdep-cov}

The second MRTS design of \sref{sec:sim-mrts-design} places the non-stationarity in the dependence through a mean-zero random effect built from two fixed cosine fields on the $[0,10]^2$ domain.  With centers $\bc_1 = (0, 10)^\top$, $\bc_2 = (7.5, 2.5)^\top$ and wavenumbers $\kappa_1 = \pi/10$, $\kappa_2 = \pi/5$,
\begin{align}
  g_j(\bs) = \cos\!\big(\kappa_j \, \| \bs - \bc_j \|\big), \qquad j = 1, 2,
  \label{eq:nsdep-basis}
\end{align}
and the random effect superposes them with independent, daily-redrawn Gaussian weights,
\begin{align}
  u_t(\bs) = \sum_{j=1}^{2} \xi_{tj}\, g_j(\bs), \qquad \xi_{tj} \iid N(0, \tau_j^2), \quad (\tau_1, \tau_2) = (5, 3),
  \label{eq:nsdep-re}
\end{align}
independently across $j$ and across days $t$.  The response adds $u_t$ to the one-knot \skewt model,
\begin{align}
  Y_t(\bs) = 10 + \lambda \, \sigma_t(\bs) \, |z_t(\bs)| + \sigma_t(\bs) \, v_t(\bs) + u_t(\bs).
  \label{eq:nsdep-gen}
\end{align}
Marginalizing over the weights, $u_t(\bs) = \sum_{j} \xi_{tj}\, g_j(\bs)$ has zero mean, $\mathrm{E}[u_t(\bs)] = \sum_j g_j(\bs)\, \mathrm{E}[\xi_{tj}] = 0$, and, by the independence of $\xi_{t1}$ and $\xi_{t2}$, the low-rank covariance
\begin{align}
  \mathrm{Cov}\big(u_t(\bs), u_t(\bs')\big)
    \;=\; \sum_{j=1}^{2} \tau_j^2\, g_j(\bs)\, g_j(\bs').
  \label{eq:nsdep-cov}
\end{align}
The pointwise variance $\mathrm{Var}\big(u_t(\bs)\big) = \sum_{j} \tau_j^2\, g_j(\bs)^2$ depends on location, so the dependence is non-stationary while the marginal mean stays constant at $10$.  Because the weights are redrawn independently across days, $u_t$ carries no temporal dependence, keeping this design orthogonal to the AR(2) experiment.
